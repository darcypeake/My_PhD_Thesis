\label{ch:conc}

The hadronic final states observed at high-energy colliders are dominated by
QCD radiation, and understanding how this radiation is organised and
described is essential for connecting theoretical predictions with
experimental measurements. This thesis has addressed this problem by
studying both how QCD radiation is organised into jets and how its
logarithmically enhanced contributions can be resummed.

In Chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS}, we introduced a new family of generalised-$k_t$
algorithms for deep-inelastic scattering, presented in
Ref.~\cite{vanBeekveld:2026rez}. We began with an overview of the jet algorithms
used in the literature, motivated in part by the absence of a comprehensive
account of DIS jet algorithms. We then described the implementation of the
algorithm in the
\texttt{DISGenkt} plugin to \textsc{FastJet}, as part of \texttt{fjcontrib},
with particular attention to issues such as IRC safety and jet ordering.
Finally, we presented phenomenological results and compared the algorithms
with the more recently developed Centauro algorithm~\cite{Arratia:2020ssx}.

The comparison highlighted differences in the treatment of soft radiation. The
algorithms differ in how much of the struck-quark radiation is retained within
the macrojet, which is reflected in the $z_{\rm jet}$ and $q_T$ distributions.
We also investigated hadronisation effects and identified observables that are
particularly insensitive to hadronisation, providing possible additional probes
of QCD. We believe that this algorithm offers an interesting avenue to re-analyse
the old HERA data, to see if it sheds any light on features missed previously.
Such a re-analysis is made particularly attractive by the fact that DIS allows
jets to be studied at different values of $Q$, providing a natural handle on the
energy dependence of non-perturbative parameters. The work also arrives at an
opportune time, with the Electron--Ion Collider (EIC) currently under
construction at Brookhaven National Laboratory and operation scheduled to begin
in the early 2030s, offering a natural setting in which the algorithm could be
applied to new data.

A further extension of this project would be the resummation of jet rates. Jet
rates contain large logarithms of the resolution parameter, so a fixed-order
prediction is unreliable in precisely the region where the behaviour of the
algorithm matters most, and resumming them is what would allow the algorithm to be
used for the $\alpha_s$ and hadronisation fits proposed above. The \textsc{ARES}
method could be used for this, requiring, as discussed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:LTS-val}, only the additional correction
$\delta\mathcal{F}_{\rm clust}$ and a single initial-state radiation leg. This
leads us to the second research project presented in this thesis.

In Chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}, we presented an extension of the \textsc{ARES}
framework to hadronic collisions. This work is directed towards a future paper,
for which we have completed the crucial conceptual stages of the extension,
something that has not been done before. We gave an overview of the \textsc{ARES}
formalism previously introduced for $\ee$ collisions, and showed that the
extension to hadronic collisions gives rise to exactly the same structure for the
master formula, with only some ingredients differing. We then derived the
treatment of initial-state radiation within the framework, validating it by
calculating the components of the master formula for one-jettiness. This
observable was chosen for its simple additive structure within \textsc{ARES}, and
because it has been resummed many times in the literature, up to N$^3$LL. Using
those results, we performed a series of direct and indirect tests, all of which
were successful.

Several extensions of this work are possible. The most immediate is the
calculation of the process-dependent hard coefficient for
$pp\to Z+\mathrm{jet}$, which would complete the ingredients required for
phenomenological predictions and allow the resummed result to be matched to
fixed-order calculations. \ABquestion{Am I showing too much that the work is 
incomplete?}
Work towards this calculation is already
underway. Beyond this, the same formalism can be applied to further
observables, opening the way to phenomenological studies of event shapes in
hadronic collisions. In particular, progress has already been made towards
the application to thrust minor and one minus transverse thrust.
