\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conc}

Theoretical predictions within QCD are of significant importance in connecting theory
to experiment. Throughout the course of this thesis, we have demonstrated exactly this
through two research projects that illustrate why such predictions are necessary to
understand more clearly what occurs within high-energy collisions at the LHC and beyond. Both
projects contribute significantly to the jet and QCD community, offering both direct
and indirect ways of impacting experimental physics.

In Chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS}, we introduced a new family of generalised-$k_t$
algorithms for deep-inelastic scattering, presented in Ref.~\cite{}. We began with
an overview of the jet algorithms used within the literature, having found during our
research that no such comprehensive overview of DIS jet algorithms currently exists.
We outlined the structure of the algorithm as implemented in the
\href{https://repo.hepforge.org/source/fastjetsvn/browse/contrib/contribs/DISGenkt}{\texttt{DISGenkt}}
plugin to \textsc{FastJet} as part of \texttt{fjcontrib}, taking special care to
consider crucial points such as IRC safety and jet ordering. We then presented
phenomenological results and compared them to the Centauro algorithm, examining the
clustering sequences of our family of algorithms alongside Centauro and studying a
range of observables that gave further insight into how the clustering formation
occurs. \add{Give reason why we look at Centauro.}

The comparison revealed clear differences in how the algorithms treat soft radiation.
The anti-$k_t$ variant clusters outward from the hardest particle, producing conical
jets whose constituents are stable against variations in the jet radius, whereas the
$k_t$ and C/A variants allow soft particles to cluster among themselves and so produce
a larger number of soft, stand-alone jets. Relative to Centauro, our algorithms retain
more of the struck-quark radiation within the macrojet, which manifests itself in a
harder $z_{\rm jet}$ spectrum and a larger fraction of events at low $q_T$. We also
looked at hadronisation effects and identified observables that are particularly
insensitive to hadronisation, which can offer possible additional tests of QCD.

We believe that this algorithm offers an exciting avenue to re-analyse the old HERA
data, to see if it sheds any light on features missed previously. Such a re-analysis
is made particularly attractive by the fact that $Q$ is measured event by event in
DIS: since non-perturbative corrections fall with inverse powers of $Q$ while
perturbative ones vary only logarithmically, a single dataset spanning a range in $Q$
offers a natural handle on the energy dependence of non-perturbative parameters, in a
way that in $\ee$ annihilation would require data taken at several beam energies. The
work also arrives at an opportune time: the Electron--Ion Collider (EIC), currently
under construction at Brookhaven National Laboratory with operation scheduled to begin
in the early 2030s, is a natural setting for this algorithm to take centre stage,
given its clean separation of the two hemispheres.

A further extension of this project would be the resummation of jet rates. Jet rates
contain large logarithms of the resolution parameter, so a fixed-order prediction is
unreliable in precisely the region where the behaviour of the algorithm matters most,
and resumming them is what would allow the algorithm to be used for the $\alpha_s$ and
hadronisation fits proposed above. The \textsc{ARES} method introduced in
Ch.~\ref{ch:hhZjet} could be used for this, where, as discussed in

In Chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}, we presented an extension of the \textsc{ARES} framework
to hadronic collisions. This work is directed towards a future paper, for which, in
this thesis, we have completed the crucial conceptual stages of extending ARES to
hadronic collisions, something that has never been done before. We have presented the
formalism and completed a mixture of direct and indirect checks to validate our
results so far, all of which have been successful. Within this chapter, we gave an
overview of the \textsc{ARES} formalism previously introduced for $\ee$ collisions,
and discussed the main result of the extension to hadronic collisions, which gives
rise to exactly the same structure for the master formula, with only some ingredients
differing. We introduced the derivation of initial-state radiation within the
\textsc{ARES} framework, validating it by calculating the components of the master
formula for the additive observable one-jettiness. We chose this observable for its
remarkably simple additive nature within the \textsc{ARES} formalism, and because it
has been resummed many times within the literature, up to N$^3$LL. Using these
results from the literature, we performed a series of direct and indirect tests, all
of which have been successful.

There are many exciting avenues in which this work could be extended. The most
obvious is to calculate the hard coefficient for the $hh\to Z+$jet process. This
would allow these coefficients to be computed numerically using the \textsc{H1jet}
programme and compared to fixed order using \textsc{Sherpa}; progress is already
being made towards this. Additionally, other observables can be calculated using this
formalism to give interesting phenomenological studies. Progress has already been
made in studying event shapes, thrust minor, and one minus transverse thrust.
