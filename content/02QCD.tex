\section{The QCD Lagrangian}
\label{sec:qCD-lang}

In the 1940s, a large number of unstable, strongly interacting particles,
collectively known as \emph{hadrons}, were discovered. In 1964, 
Gell-Mann~\cite{Gell-Mann:1964ewy} and Zweig~\cite{Zweig:1964ruk, Zweig:1964jf} 
independently proposed the quark model, in which hadrons are interpreted as composite objects built from 
fundamental spin-1/2 constituents known as \emph{quarks}. The six known quarks are organised into three generations -- light, intermediate, and heavy -- as shown in Table~\ref{table:quark-properties}.

Hadrons are broadly classified into two categories: mesons ($q\bar{q}$), which are 
integer-spin bosons, and baryons ($qqq$), which are half-integer-spin fermions. 
Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is the $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ non-Abelian gauge theory 
governing the strong interaction. It describes how quarks bind together to form hadrons,
with the interaction mediated by gauge bosons known as gluons. Throughout this thesis, 
quarks and gluons are collectively referred to as \emph{partons} and carry a conserved 
charge known as colour.

Quarks transform in the fundamental representation of $\mathrm{SU}(3)$,
with fields denoted by $q_a$, where $a=1,2,3$ labels the three colour
states, conventionally referred to as red, green, and blue. The corresponding
antiquark fields are denoted by $\bar{q}^a$ and transform in the conjugate
representation, where $\bar{q}=q^\dagger\gamma^0$ is the Dirac adjoint of
$q$. Gluons, denoted by $\mathcal{A}_\mu^A$, transform in the adjoint
representation of $\mathrm{SU}(3)$, with $A=1,\ldots,8$ labelling the eight
colour degrees of freedom of the gluon field.
 
 The QCD Lagrangian density governing the dynamics of quarks with mass $m_f$ 
 and massless gluons can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: L-QCD}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QCD}}
= \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{classical}}
+ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GF}}
+ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ghost}}\,.
\end{equation}
The classical contribution contains the kinetic and interaction terms 
for the quark and gluon fields,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: L-Classical}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{classical}}
= -\frac{1}{4} F_{\mu\nu}^{A} F^{\mu\nu}_{A}
+ \sum_{f=1}^{n_f} \bar{q}^f_a (i\slashed{D} - m_f)_{ab}\, q^f_b \,.
\end{equation}
Here $n_f$ denotes the number of quark flavours, with $n_f = 6$ in the Standard Model 
(see Table~\ref{table:quark-properties}). The gluon dynamics are encoded in the non-Abelian field-strength tensor
\begin{equation}
F_{\alpha\beta}^{A}
= \partial_\alpha \mathcal{A}_\beta^A
- \partial_\beta \mathcal{A}_\alpha^A
- g_s f^{ABC} \mathcal{A}_\alpha^B \mathcal{A}_\beta^C \,,
\end{equation}
where $g_s$ is the strong coupling constant, conventionally expressed through
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_s \equiv \frac{g_s^2}{4\pi}\,.
    \label{eq:coupling-constant}
\end{equation}
The covariant derivative is written using Feynman slash notation, $\slashed{D} = \gamma_\mu D^\mu$, with
\begin{equation}
(D_\mu)_{ab}
= \partial_\mu \delta_{ab}
+ ig_s (t^C \mathcal{A}_\mu^C)_{ab}\,.
\end{equation}
The gamma matrices satisfy the anti-commutation relation $\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\} = 2 g^{\mu\nu}$
and throughout this work we adopt the metric convention $g^{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$.

The matrices $t^C$ are the generators of the fundamental representation of $\mathrm{SU}(3)$, 
given explicitly by 
\begin{equation}
	t^C = \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^C\,,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda^C$ are the eight traceless Hermitian Gell-Mann matrices. 
These satisfy the commutation relation
\begin{equation}
[t^A, t^B] = i f^{ABC} t^C\,,
\end{equation}
where $f^{ABC}$ are the structure constants of the $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ gauge group.
The generators satisfy the Casimir identities
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\mathrm{Tr}(t^A t^B) = T_R\, \delta^{AB}, \qquad T_R = \tfrac{1}{2}, \\[1ex]
&t^{A}_{ab} t^{A}_{bc} = C_F\, \delta_{ac}, \qquad \qquad C_F = \tfrac{4}{3}, \\[1ex]
&f^{ABC} f^{ABD} = C_A\, \delta^{CD}, \quad C_A = 3\,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where repeated indices are summed implicitly.

The second term in Eq.~\eqref{eq: L-QCD} is the gauge-fixing contribution,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GF}}
    = -\frac{1}{2\xi} \left( \partial^\mu \mathcal{A}_\mu^{A} \right)^{2}\,,
\end{equation}
which is required to remove redundant gauge degrees of freedom 
associated with the local $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ gauge invariance of the QCD
Lagrangian. The parameter $\xi$ specifies the choice of gauge: for example, 
$\xi = 1$ corresponds to the Feynman gauge, while $\xi = 0$ corresponds to the Landau gauge.

The final term in Eq.~\eqref{eq: L-QCD} is the ghost contribution,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{ghost} = \partial_\mu \eta^{A^\dagger}(D_{AB}^\mu\,\eta^B)\,,
\end{equation}
where $\eta^A$ are complex scalar fields known as Faddeev--Popov ghosts. 
These fields anti-commute and therefore obey Fermi statistics. 
Ghosts do not correspond to physical states and instead arise as a
consequence of the gauge-fixing procedure in non-Abelian gauge theories.

The Feynman rules obtained from the QCD Lagrangian are illustrated in
Figs.~\ref{fig:QCD-Propagator-Rules} and~\ref{fig:QCD-Vertex-Rules}. The propagators arise from the kinetic terms,

\section{Perturbation theory}
\label{sec:pert-theory}

With the QCD Lagrangian and its Feynman rules established, we now outline how
perturbative calculations are performed in practice. Scattering processes are
described by considering all Feynman diagrams contributing to the transition
between a given set of incoming and outgoing states. Each diagram is constructed
from the propagators and interaction vertices shown in
Figs.~\ref{fig:QCD-Propagator-Rules} and~\ref{fig:QCD-Vertex-Rules}, and translated
into a mathematical expression according to the corresponding Feynman rules.
The sum of all contributing diagrams gives the \emph{scattering amplitude}, or
matrix element, $\mathcal{M}$. Cross sections are
then obtained from the squared amplitude, integrated over the allowed phase
space.

To illustrate the construction of a partonic cross section, consider a hard
$2\to m$ scattering process involving massless partons,
\[
    p_a + p_b \rightarrow p_1 + \cdots + p_m\,.
\]
The corresponding partonic cross section is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:2-to-m-xsec}
   \hat{ \sigma}= \frac{1}{2s}\int d\Phi^{(m)}(p_1,\ldots,p_m)\,|\mathcal{M}_m(p_a,p_b; p_1,\ldots,p_m)|^2\,F_J^{(m)}(p_1,\ldots,p_m)\,,
\end{equation}
where the individual components are defined as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $1/(2s)$ is the Lorentz-invariant flux factor for the two
  massless incoming partons, with $s=(p_a+p_b)^2$ the
  centre-of-mass energy squared of the hard process;
  
    \item $d\Phi^{(m)}$ denotes the $m$-parton phase space measure, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:m-3-phase-space}
d\Phi^{(m)} = \left[\prod_{i=1}^{m} \frac{d^3 p_i}{(2\pi)^3\, 2E_i}\right]
\left(2\pi\right)^4\delta^{(4)}\Big(p_a + p_b - \sum_{i=1}^{m} p_i\Big)\,,
\end{equation}

\item $|\mathcal{M}_m|^2 = \langle\mathcal{M}_m|\mathcal{M}_m\rangle$
  is the squared scattering amplitude for the $2\to m$ process. The
   inner product includes a sum over the colour and spin
  degrees of freedom of all final-state partons and an average
  over those of the initial-state partons;

\item $F_J^{(m)}$ is the \emph{jet function}, which specifies the observable under consideration.
Its precise form depends on the observable
and may contain $\Theta$-functions, $\delta$-functions, or numerical
factors. The choice $F_J^{(m)} = 1$ reduces Eq.~\eqref{eq:2-to-m-xsec} to the total partonic cross section. 
\end{itemize}

The structure of the QCD interaction vertices implies that additional gluon
emissions or internal loops introduce further powers of the strong coupling
$\alpha_s$. The partonic cross section can therefore be organised as a
perturbative expansion in $\alpha_s$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sig-pert-theory}
  \hat\sigma =
  \hat\sigma^{(0)}
  +\frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}\,\hat\sigma^{(1)}
  +\left(\frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}\right)^2\hat\sigma^{(2)}
  +\dots\,,
\end{equation}
where $\hat\sigma^{(0)}$ is the tree-level contribution, referred to as the
leading-order (LO) or Born term. The terms
$\hat\sigma^{(1)},\hat\sigma^{(2)},\ldots$ represent successive orders in the
perturbative expansion, known as next-to-leading order (NLO),
next-to-next-to-leading order (NNLO), and so on.

As we will see in Sec.~\ref{sec:running-coupling}, $\alpha_s$ becomes small at
high energies. This means that successive terms in the perturbative expansion
are typically suppressed in this regime. As a result, the expansion can be well
approximated by retaining only the first few terms, yielding a \emph{fixed-order}
(FO) prediction. This approach is known as perturbation theory, and its
systematic application to QCD processes forms the basis of perturbative QCD
(pQCD). Throughout this thesis, all calculations are performed within the
framework of pQCD.

\section{The running of the strong coupling $\alpha_s$}
\label{sec:running-coupling}

As discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:pert-theory}, the validity of pQCD 
relies on the strong coupling becoming small at high energies. 
This behaviour follows from the fact that $\alpha_s$ is not a fixed constant,
but instead \emph{runs} with the renormalisation scale $\mu_R$ 
-- an unphysical scale introduced by the renormalisation procedure, discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:divergences}. 
The scale dependence of the coupling is governed by the renormalisation group equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coupling-DE}
    \mu_R^2 \frac{\partial \alpha_s(\mu_R^2)}{\partial \mu_R^2} = \beta(\alpha_s)\,,
\end{equation}
where the QCD $\beta$-function admits a perturbative expansion of the form
\begin{equation}
    \beta(\alpha_s) = -\left(\beta_0\alpha_s^2 + \beta_1\alpha_s^3 + \beta_2\alpha_s^4 + \cdots\right)\,.
\end{equation}
The first two coefficients are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:beta}
\begin{align}
\label{eq:beta0}
    \beta_0 &= \frac{11 C_A - 4T_Rn_f}{12\pi}\,,\\[1em]
    \label{eq:beta1}
   \beta_1 &= \frac{17C_A^2 - 10C_A T_R n_f - 6C_F T_Rn_f}{24\pi^2}\,.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The coefficients $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ are renormalisation-scheme
independent, while higher coefficients depend on the choice of
renormalisation scheme, taken throughout to be the $\overline{\mathrm{MS}}$
scheme (discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:UV-div}).

 Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:coupling-DE} at leading order with reference scale $Q$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:scale-dependence}
    \alpha_s(\mu_R^2)
    = \frac{\alpha_s(Q^2)}
    {1 + \alpha_s(Q^2)\,\beta_0\,\ln\!\left(\tfrac{\mu_R^2}{Q^2}\right)}\, .
\end{equation}
The sign of $\beta_0$ and the form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:scale-dependence} determine
the scale dependence of the strong coupling.
As $\mu_R \to \infty$, the coupling decreases, and quarks and gluons behave increasingly like free particles. 
This behaviour is known as \emph{asymptotic freedom}, a regime in which pQCD provides an excellent approximation. 
Conversely, as $\mu_R$ decreases towards
$\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}$, a scale of a few hundred MeV,
the coupling grows large, signalling the breakdown of perturbation theory and 
the emergence of a strongly interacting regime in which quarks and gluons are confined into hadrons. 
This phenomenon is known as \emph{confinement}. 

The running of $\alpha_s$ has been confirmed experimentally across
several orders of magnitude in $Q$, as illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:strong-coupling}, with the five-loop QCD prediction
in excellent agreement with data from a wide range of processes.
Throughout this thesis we work in the high-energy regime
$\mu_R\gg\Lambda_{\rm QCD}$, thereby exploiting asymptotic freedom.

\section{Divergences}
\label{sec:divergences}

While perturbation theory is very effective at high energies, individual
contributions beyond leading order introduce divergences that must be treated 
systematically. In QCD, these divergences arise in two distinct forms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ultraviolet (UV) divergences} arise in loop diagrams when the momentum of the
    virtual particle becomes large.
    \item \textbf{Infrared (IR) divergences} arise when a gluon emission becomes soft (low energy) or
    collinear to a hard (high energy) parton.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{UV divergences}
\label{subsec:UV-div}

One Feynman rule not shown in
Figs.~\ref{fig:QCD-Propagator-Rules} and~\ref{fig:QCD-Vertex-Rules}
is associated with loop diagrams. Each independent loop momentum
introduces an integration of the form
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{loop} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{d^4q}{(2\pi)^4}\,,
\end{equation}
where $q^\mu$ denotes the momentum of the virtual particle running inside the loop, as
illustrated by the fermion bubble diagram in Fig.~\ref{fig:fermion-bubble}.

Taking the quarks to be massless, as assumed throughout this thesis, the Feynman
rules give an integral of the form
\begin{equation}
    I = \int d^4 q \, \frac{N^{\mu\nu}}{q^2 (p+q)^2}\,,
\end{equation}
where $N^{\mu\nu}$ denotes a numerator whose explicit form is not required for the present
discussion. At large loop momentum, the integral reveals a UV divergence,
\begin{equation}
    I \sim \lim_{\Lambda \to \infty} \int_0^\Lambda \frac{dq}{q}
      \sim \lim_{\Lambda \to \infty} \ln \Lambda\,.
\end{equation}
Such divergences cannot be allowed to remain in the theory, 
and therefore a systematic method for handling them is required. 
This is achieved through a procedure known as \emph{regularisation}. 
Although several regularisation procedures  exist, in this thesis we use \emph{dimensional regularisation}~\cite{tHooft:1972tcz, Bollini:1972ui}. This method preserves both Lorentz and gauge invariance and, as will be discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:IR-div}, can also be applied to infrared divergences. The basic idea is to promote the space-time dimension to $d = 4 - 2\varepsilon$, such that 
logarithmic divergences appear as poles in $\varepsilon$. 

Once these poles have been isolated, the next step is to remove them. This is achieved by 
a procedure known as \emph{renormalisation} where the parameters of the theory are redefined 
to absorb the divergent contributions via counterterms. Throughout this thesis we work in the modified minimal subtraction ($\overline{\mathrm{MS}}$) renormalisation scheme~\cite{Bardeen:1978yd}.
In this scheme, the counterterms subtract not only the $1/\varepsilon$ poles, 
but also the accompanying constants $\ln 4\pi$ and $\gamma_E \approx 0.5772$ (the Euler--Mascheroni constant), 
that arise from using dimensional regularisation. The renormalisation procedure introduces the scale $\mu_R$, giving rise to the corresponding scale dependence of the strong coupling $\alpha_s$, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:running-coupling}.

\subsection{IR divergences}
\label{subsec:IR-div}

We now turn to infrared (IR) divergences, which arise from the emission
of partons in the soft and collinear limits. To illustrate their
origin and cancellation, we consider the simplest QCD process,
$e^+e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{hadrons}$.  At leading order, the hadronic
cross section is described by the corresponding partonic process
$e^+e^- \rightarrow \gamma^* \rightarrow q\bar{q}$, shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:photon-qq}. This approach is
justified by quark--hadron duality~\cite{Poggio:1975af}: in the high-energy regime, the \emph{fully inclusive} hadronic cross
section can be approximated sufficiently well by the corresponding partonic cross
section computed in perturbation theory \footnote{For differential distributions, one expects a more pronounced difference between parton and hadron level. However, it is found that hadronisation does not significantly reshuffle the momentum of very energetic partons, a feature known as local parton-hadron duality Ref.~\cite{Shifman:2000jv, Azimov:1984np}.}.

The corresponding leading-order cross section is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sigma-LO}
	\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}=\frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{3s}N_c\sum_fQ_f^2\,,
\end{equation}
where $Q_f$ is the electric charge of a quark of flavour $f$,
$N_c=3$ is the number of quark colours, $s=(p_{e^+}+p_{e^-})^2$ is the
centre-of-mass energy squared, and $\alpha$ is the QED coupling.

corrections. We first consider the real-emission contribution,

Writing the three-parton phase space in terms of the energy fractions
\begin{equation}
x_i = \frac{2E_i}{\sqrt{s}}\,,
\qquad i=q,\bar{q},g\,,
\end{equation}
the corresponding cross section is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qqg-real}
\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}_{\rm R}
=
\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}
C_F\frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}
\int_0^1 dx_q
\int_0^1 dx_{\bar{q}}\,
\frac{x_q^2+x_{\bar{q}}^2}
{(1-x_q)(1-x_{\bar{q}})}
\Theta\left(x_q+x_{\bar{q}}-1\right),
\end{equation}
where energy conservation gives
$\sum_i x_i=2$. The Heaviside function enforces the kinematic
constraint $x_q+x_{\bar{q}}\geq1$, which follows directly from energy conservation in the three-particle final state.

The singular structure is apparent from the integrand in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qqg-real}. In particular, divergences arise in the
following limits:
\begin{itemize}
\item $x_q\to1$ and $x_{\bar{q}}\to1$ simultaneously, corresponding
to a soft gluon, $E_g\to0$;
\item $x_q\to1$, corresponding to the antiquark and gluon becoming
collinear, $\theta_{\bar{q}g}\to0$;
\item $x_{\bar{q}}\to1$, corresponding to the quark and gluon becoming
collinear, $\theta_{qg}\to0$.
\end{itemize}
Here, $\theta_{qg}$ and $\theta_{\bar{q}g}$ denote the quark--gluon and
antiquark--gluon angles, respectively. These soft and collinear singularities are 
collectively referred to as IR (or IRC) divergences. 

To regulate these divergences, we evaluate this integral in
$d=4-2\varepsilon$ dimensions using dimensional regularisation.
(see Sec.~\ref{subsec:UV-div}).\footnote{Note that the $m$-particle
phase space in Eq.~\eqref{eq:m-3-phase-space}, is written as
$d$ dimensions as $d\Phi^{(m)}=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d^d p_i}{(2\pi)^d}(2\pi)\delta(p_i^2)\Theta(p_i^0)\right]
(2\pi)^d\delta^{(d)}\left(p_a+p_b-\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_j\right)\,.$} 
The regulated expression is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qqg-real-d-dim}
\begin{aligned}
	\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}_{\rm R}=&\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}\,C_F\frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}\left(\frac{4\pi\mu_R^2}{s}\right)^\varepsilon\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\varepsilon)}\int_0^1dx_q\int_0^1dx_{\bar{q}}\\
	&\times\frac{(x_q^2+x_{\bar{q}}^2)-\varepsilon(2-x_q-x_{\bar{q}})^2}{(1-x_q)^{1+\varepsilon}\,(1-x_{\bar{q}})^{1+\varepsilon}\,(x_q+x_{\bar{q}}-1)^\varepsilon}\,\Theta\left(x_q+x_{\bar{q}}-1\right)\,.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Performing the integrations gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qqg-real-d-dim-more}
    \hat{\sigma}^{(1)}_{\rm R}
    = \sigma_0\, \frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}\,
      \frac{C_F}{\Gamma(1-\varepsilon)}
      \left(\frac{4\pi\mu_R^2}{s}\right)^{\varepsilon}
      \left(
          \frac{2}{\varepsilon^2}
        + \frac{3}{\varepsilon}
        - \pi^2
        + \frac{19}{2}

      \right).
\end{equation}

The virtual contribution is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\sigma}^{(1)}_{\rm V}
    = \sigma_0\, \frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}\,
      \frac{C_F}{\Gamma(1-\varepsilon)}
      \left(\frac{4\pi\mu_R^2}{s}\right)^{\varepsilon}
      \left(
         -\frac{2}{\varepsilon^2}
         -\frac{3}{\varepsilon}
         + \pi^2
         - 8
         + \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
      \right)\,.
\end{equation}
The poles are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign to those appearing
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:qqg-real-d-dim-more}. Consequently, the sum of the real
and virtual contributions is finite and is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Real-plus-Virt}
\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}_{\rm R}+ \hat{\sigma}^{(1)}_{\rm V}
= \hat{\sigma}^{(0)}\,\frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}\,
\frac{C_F}{\Gamma(1-\varepsilon)}
\left(\frac{4\pi\mu_R^2}{s}\right)^{\varepsilon}
\left(\frac{3}{2} + \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\right)\,.
\end{equation}
Expanding in $\varepsilon$ and taking the limit $\varepsilon\to0$,
the total cross section up to NLO accuracy is consequently
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sigma-full}
\hat{\sigma}
= \hat{\sigma}^{(0)}
\left(
1 + \frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}\,\frac{3}{2} C_F
+ \mathcal{O}(\alpha_s^2)
\right)\,.
\end{equation}
The cancellation of the IR singularities between the real and virtual
contributions is a direct consequence of the \emph{KLN theorem}
\cite{Kinoshita:1962ur, Lee:1964is}, which guarantees the cancellation
of IR divergences between real and virtual contributions 
order by order in perturbation theory provided one sums
over all degenerate initial and final states.

\section{Soft and collinear factorisation of $|\mathcal{M}_m|^2$}
\label{sec:M-factorisation}

In Sec.~\ref{subsec:IR-div}, we illustrated the cancellation of infrared
singularities between real and virtual corrections for $e^+e^- \to
\mathrm{hadrons}$ at NLO. We now consider the general structure of these
divergences by examining the behaviour of the squared matrix element
$|\mathcal{M}_m|^2$ in the soft and collinear limits, where it factorises into a
remarkably simple and universal form~\cite{Catani:1999ss}. It is this
factorisation that underpins the systematic treatment of IR singularities in
QCD.

\subsection{Soft limit}
\label{subsec:soft-fact}

Suppressing the initial-state momenta $(p_a,p_b)$ for brevity, consider
the emission of an additional soft parton with momentum $q$. In the
soft limit, the squared tree-level matrix element factorises as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:soft-fact}
    |\mathcal{M}_{m+1}^{(0)}(q,p_1,\ldots,p_m)|^2
    \simeq
    -4\pi\alpha_s\mu_R^{2\varepsilon}
    \sum_{i,j=1}^{m}
        S_{ij}(q)\,
        \left\langle
            \mathcal{M}_m^{(0)}(p_1,\ldots,p_m)
        \left|
            \mathbf{T}_i \cdot \mathbf{T}_j
        \right|
            \mathcal{M}_m^{(0)}(p_1,\ldots,p_m)
        \right\rangle\,,
\end{equation}
where the superscript $^{(0)}$ denotes the tree-level matrix element.
The colour-charge operators $\mathbf{T}_i$ act on the colour indices
of parton $i$ and satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:colour-charge-op}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{T}_i \cdot \mathbf{T}_j &= \mathbf{T}_j \cdot \mathbf{T}_i\,,
    \qquad (i\neq j),\\[4pt]
    \mathbf{T}_i^2 &= C_i\,,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $C_i = C_F$ for quarks and $C_i = C_A$ for gluons. 
The eikonal factor $S_{ij}(q)$ contains the kinematic dependence of
the soft emission and is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eikonal-fac}
    S_{ij}(q) = \frac{2p_i \cdot p_j}{(2p_i \cdot q)(2p_j \cdot q)} \,.
\end{equation}

colour charges and directions of the hard legs, not on the details

\subsection{Collinear limit}
\label{subsec:collinear-fact}

We next consider the limit in which two partons become collinear.
The momenta of the two partons, $i$ and $j$, can be parametrised using a Sudakov
decomposition,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    p_i^\mu &= z\, p^\mu + k_\perp^\mu
               - \frac{k_\perp^2}{z}\,\frac{n^\mu}{2p\cdot n}\,, \\
    p_j^\mu &= (1-z)\,p^\mu - k_\perp^\mu
               - \frac{k_\perp^2}{1-z}\,\frac{n^\mu}{2p\cdot n}\,,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $p^\mu$ is a light-like vector ($p^2 = 0$) defining the collinear 
direction, $n^\mu$ is an auxiliary light-like vector ($n^2 = 0$), and $k_\perp^\mu$ 
is the transverse momentum ($k_\perp^2 < 0$, $k_\perp \cdot p = k_\perp \cdot n = 0$).
The parameter $z$ is the longitudinal momentum fraction carried by parton $i$,
and the collinear limit corresponds to $k_\perp^\mu\to0$.

In this limit, the squared matrix element factorises as 
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  |\mathcal{M}_{m+1}^{(0)}&(p_1,\ldots,p_i,p_j,\ldots,p_{m+1})|^2
  \simeq\\
  &\frac{8\pi\alpha_s\mu_R^{2\varepsilon}}{s_{ij}}
  \left\langle\mathcal{M}_m^{(0)}(p_1,\ldots,p_{ij},\ldots,p_m)
  \left|\hat{P}_{(ij)\to i +j}(z,k_\perp;\varepsilon)\right|
  \mathcal{M}_m^{(0)}(p_1,\ldots,p_{ij},\ldots,p_m)
  \right\rangle\,,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $p_{ij}^\mu = p_i^\mu + p_j^\mu$ is the momentum of the parent 
parton $(ij)$ and $s_{ij}\equiv (p_i + p_j)^2$. The term $\hat{P}_{(ij) \to i + j}(z,k_\perp;\varepsilon)$ is the unregularised
tree-level Altarelli--Parisi splitting function for the collinear
splitting $ij\to i+j$. The explicit forms are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:AP-functions}
\begin{aligned}
  &\hat{P}_{g\to q\bar{q}}^{\mu\nu} (z,k_\perp;\varepsilon)=
   \hat{P}_{g\to \bar{q}q}^{\mu\nu} (z,k_\perp;\varepsilon)=
  T_R\!\left[-g^{\mu\nu}+4z(1-z)
  \frac{k_\perp^\mu k_\perp^\nu}{k_\perp^2}\right]\,,\\[1ex]
  &\hat{P}_{q\to qg}^{ss'} (z,k_\perp;\varepsilon)=
  \hat{P}_{\bar{q}\to \bar{q}g}^{ss'} (z,k_\perp;\varepsilon)=
  \delta_{ss'}\,C_F\!\left[\frac{1+z^2}{1-z}
  -\varepsilon(1-z)\right]\,,\\[1ex]
  &\hat{P}_{q\to gq}^{ss'} (z,k_\perp;\varepsilon)=
   \hat{P}_{\bar{q}\to g\bar{q}}^{ss'} (z,k_\perp;\varepsilon)=
  \delta_{ss'}\,C_F\!\left[\frac{1+(1-z)^2}{z}
  -\varepsilon z\right]\,,\\[1ex]
 &\hat{P}_{g\to gg}^{\mu\nu} (z,k_\perp;\varepsilon)=
  2C_A\!\left[-g^{\mu\nu}\!\left(\frac{z}{1-z}+\frac{1-z}{z}\right)
  -2(1-\varepsilon)z(1-z)
  \frac{k_\perp^\mu k_\perp^\nu}{k_\perp^2}\right]\,,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the gluon splitting functions $\hat{P}^{\mu\nu}$ carry Lorentz
indices $\mu,\nu$ corresponding to the two polarisation states
of the parent gluon, while the quark splitting functions
$\hat{P}^{ss'}$ carry spin indices $s,s'$ of the parent
fermion. In both cases the indices are contracted with those of the reduced
matrix element in the factorisation formula above.

\section{IRC Safety} 
\label{sec:IRC}

In Sec.~\ref{sec:divergences}, we saw through the explicit NLO calculation
that the infrared singularities arising from
real and virtual contributions cancel, yielding a finite result. This
cancellation relies on the fact that the total cross section is a
\emph{fully inclusive} observable, for which $F_J^{(m)} = 1$ for any
number of final-state particles (see Sec.~\ref{sec:pert-theory}).

For more general observables, such a cancellation is not guaranteed.
To obtain finite perturbative predictions, the observable must be
\emph{infrared and collinear safe} (IRC safe): its value must be insensitive 
to the emission of additional soft and/or collinear partons.
At NLO, these conditions can be expressed in terms of the jet function as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&F_J^{(m+1)}(p_1,\ldots,p_i,\ldots,p_{m+1})
\;\longrightarrow\;
F_J^{(m)}(p_1,\ldots,\cancel{p_i},\ldots,p_{m+1})\,,
\qquad\qquad\qquad E_i \to 0, \\[6pt]
&F_J^{(m+1)}(p_1,\ldots,p_i,p_j,\ldots,p_{m+1})
\;\longrightarrow\;
F_J^{(m)}(p_1,\ldots,\cancel{p_i},\cancel{p_j},p_{ij},\ldots,p_{m+1})\,,
\quad p_i \parallel p_j\,,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:IRC-safety}
\end{equation}
with a diagrammatic representation shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:IRC_safety}.

Although Eq.~\eqref{eq:IRC-safety} is written for a single additional parton, the same condition extends to any number of soft or collinear partons. From this definition it follows immediately that fully inclusive observables with $F_J^{(m)} = 1$ are IRC safe. A simple example of an IRC-unsafe observable is the number of final-state partons. 

Although IRC safety ensures a finite result at every order in perturbation theory, it does not guarantee a well-behaved perturbative expansion. Many observables, while IRC safe, do not display a complete cancellation between real and virtual contributions -- only enough to remove divergences. This incomplete cancellation generates large logarithmic terms arising from emissions in the soft and/or collinear regions. Such quantities are referred to as \emph{semi-inclusive} observables~\cite{Bassetto:1983mvz}. A prominent class of examples are event-shape variables, discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:resum}. These logarithms can spoil the convergent nature of the perturbative expansion and must be \emph{resummed} -- the subject of Chapters~\ref{ch:resum} and~\ref{ch:hhZjet}. 

\section{The full picture: high-energy collisions}
\label{sec:high-energy-picture}

Up to this point, the discussion has largely focused on physics at the parton level.
In particular, we have relied on concepts such as quark--hadron duality (cf.\ Sec.~\ref{sec:divergences}) to relate partonic cross sections to hadronic observables, and have worked predominantly in the high-energy regime, where asymptotic freedom (cf.\ Sec.~\ref{sec:running-coupling}) allows the use of  perturbative QCD. 

In realistic collider experiments, however, the situation is significantly more complex.
Consider, for example, a hadronic collision at the LHC. The incoming particles are composite hadrons rather than elementary partons, and the final-state particles observed in the detector consist of hadrons produced at energy scales far below that of the hard interaction. A complete description of such a collision therefore necessarily involves physics beyond the perturbative regime. 

The aim of this section is to outline the various stages of a high-energy hadronic collision,
 organised by the relevant energy scale $Q$. These stages are depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:high-energy-event}, 
which shows a $t\bar{t}h$ event produced in a proton--proton collision 
as generated by the \textsc{Sherpa}~\cite{Gleisberg:2008ta} collaboration.

\subsection{Hard scattering $(Q \gg \Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}})$}
\label{subsec:hard-scattering}

At the highest energy scale, the dynamics of the collision are governed by the hard scattering, represented by the central red blob in Fig.~\ref{fig:high-energy-event}. In this process, two partons, denoted $i$ and $j$, are extracted from the incoming protons $P_1$ and $P_2$ and undergo a short-distance interaction. The partonic content of the proton is encoded in parton distribution functions (PDFs), $f_{i/P}(x,Q^2)$, which give the probability of finding a parton of flavour $i$ carrying longitudinal momentum fraction $x$ of the proton $P$ at the energy scale $Q^2$.  

PDFs are non-perturbative functions and must be extracted from experimental data.
However, their scale dependence is governed by perturbative QCD through the Dokshitzer--Gribov--Lipatov--Altarelli--Parisi (DGLAP) evolution equations~\cite{Gribov:1972ri,Dokshitzer:1977sg,Altarelli:1977zs}, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:DGLAP}
    \frac{\partial f_{i/P}(x,Q^2)}{\partial\ln Q^2}
    = \sum_{j} \int_x^1 \frac{dz}{z}\,
      \frac{\alpha_s(Q^2)}{2\pi}\,
      P_{ij}(z,\alpha_s(Q^2))\,
      f_{j/P}\!\left(\frac{x}{z}, Q^2\right),
\end{equation}
where the Altarelli-Parisi splitting functions $P_{ij}(z)$ admit the perturbative expansion
\begin{equation}
	P_{ij}(z,\alpha_s(Q^2)) = P_{ij}^{(0)}(z)+\frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi} P_{ij}^{(1)}(z)+\dots\,.
\end{equation}
\ndp{The leading-order splitting functions $P_{ij}^{(0)}$ are the
regularised counterparts of the unregularised functions
$\hat{P}_{(ij)\to i+j}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:AP-functions}: they are
obtained by integrating $\hat{P}$ over the collinear phase space,
applying the $(1-z)_+$ prescription to handle the soft $z\to1$
divergence, and adding the virtual $\delta(1-z)$ piece from
one-loop corrections. Explicitly,}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:splitting-func-real-virt}
\begin{aligned}
&P_{qq}^{(0)}(z)=C_F\left[\frac{1+z^2}{(1-z)_+}+\frac{3}{2}\delta(1-z)\right]\,,\\
&P_{qg}^{(0)}(z)=T_R\left[z^2+(1-z)^2\right]\,,\\
&P_{gq}^{(0)}(z)=C_F\left[\frac{1+(1-z)^2}{z}\right]\,,\\
&P_{gg}^{(0)}(z)=2C_A\left[\frac{z}{(1-z)_+}+\frac{1-z}{z}+z(1-z)\right]+2\pi\beta_0\delta(1-z)\,.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\ABquestion{Are these initial or final-state splittings?}
\ndp{The $(1-z)_+$ prescription is defined by
$\int_0^1 dz\,f(z)/(1-z)_+ = \int_0^1 dz\,(f(z)-f(1))/(1-z)$
and ensures integrability at $z\to1$. The $\delta(1-z)$ terms in
$P_{qq}^{(0)}$ and $P_{gg}^{(0)}$ arise from virtual corrections

The hard interaction produces the primary particles of the event -- in this case a $t\bar{t}h$ system, indicated by the smaller red blobs in Fig.~\ref{fig:high-energy-event}. Since the energy scale $Q$ is much larger than $\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}$, predictions at this stage can be reliably obtained using fixed-order perturbation theory (Sec.~\ref{sec:pert-theory}). Such calculations are typically provided by matrix-element generators such as \textsc{MadGraph}~\cite{Alwall:2014hca} and \textsc{MCFM}~\cite{Campbell:1999ah,Campbell:2011bn,Campbell:2019dru}.

\subsection{QCD evolution $(Q \gtrsim \Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}})$}
\label{subsec:QCD-ev}

Following the hard scattering, the coloured partons produced in the event undergo further QCD radiation, illustrated by the red quark and gluon propagators in Fig.~\ref{fig:high-energy-event}. This radiation is dominated by soft and collinear emissions, which for many observables give rise to logarithmically enhanced contributions (see Sec.~\ref{sec:IRC}). When such logarithms become large, fixed-order perturbation theory is no longer sufficient, and dedicated techniques are required to obtain reliable results in the perturbative regime. The accuracy of these methods is typically characterised in terms of logarithmic accuracy, such as leading logarithmic (LL), next-to-leading logarithmic (NLL), and so on. Common approaches include:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Parton showers} model the
	soft and collinear emissions from the hard scale down to the hadronisation scale. 
	Well-known implementations include \textsc{Sherpa}~\cite{Gleisberg:2008ta}, \textsc{Pythia}~\cite{Bierlich:2022pfr}, \textsc{Herwig}~\cite{Bahr:2008pv, Bellm:2015jjp}, and \textsc{PanScales}~\cite{vanBeekveld:2023ivn}. While \textsc{Pythia} and \textsc{Herwig} achieve LL accuracy, the \textsc{Sherpa}~\cite{Herren:2022jej, Sherpa:2024mfk} and \textsc{PanScales}~\cite{vanBeekveld:2022zhl, vanBeekveld:2022ukn,vanBeekveld:2023chs} showers have demonstrated NLL accuracy for a broad class of processes and observables, with ongoing work aimed at extending to higher logarithmic accuracy. 
	
\item \emph{QCD resummation} systematically resums logarithmically 
enhanced contributions from soft and collinear radiation to all 
orders in perturbation theory. Semi-numerical
resummation frameworks such as \textsc{CAESAR}~\cite{Banfi:2004yd} and \textsc{ARES}~\cite{Banfi:2014sua, Banfi:2016zlc, Banfi:2018mcq, Arpino:2019ozn} fall into this category. 
For $e^+e^-$, resummation for jet observables has been achieved at 
    NNLL accuracy, while for hadronic collisions 
    the state of the art is generally NLL. The work presented in 
    Chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet} focuses on extending the \textsc{ARES} 
    framework to hadronic collisions at NNLL accuracy.

\item \emph{Effective field theory (EFT) resummation}, most notably through soft-collinear effective theory (SCET)~\cite{Bauer:2000ew, Bauer:2000yr, Bauer:2001ct, Bauer:2001yt}, describes soft and collinear degrees of freedom through a systematic observable-dependent factorisation formula. In this framework, the relevant dynamics are encoded in separate hard, collinear, soft, and jet functions, which enables the resummation to be carried out analytically for a wide range of observables. In some cases, this can be performed up to $\mathrm{N^3LL}$ accuracy, as demonstrated in Refs.~\cite{Becher:2008cf, Abbate:2010xh, Alioli:2023rxx}. 
	\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hadronisation ($Q \sim \Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}$)}
\label{subsec:hadronisation}

As the energy scale of the event approaches $\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}$,  confinement effects dominate and hadronisation occurs. The coloured partons produced by the hard scattering and subsequent QCD evolution (light green blobs in Fig.~\ref{fig:high-energy-event}) are transformed into colour-singlet hadrons (dark green blobs). These primary hadrons subsequently decay into longer-lived particles (smaller dark green blobs) that are ultimately observed in the detector. Experimentally, these particles appear predominantly as \emph{jets} -- collimated sprays of hadrons -- whose clustering properties are discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:jet-phys}. Since hadronisation occurs in the 
non-perturbative regime, pQCD cannot be applied directly; instead, its 
effects are modelled using phenomenological approaches such as the 
string~\cite{Sjostrand:1982fn,Andersson:1983jt} and 
cluster~\cite{Webber:1983if} models, as well as analytical 
power-correction methods~\cite{Dokshitzer:1995qm}.

The three stages described above capture the main features of the QCD dynamics in a high-energy hadronic collision, but several additional ingredients contribute to the structure of a realistic event. Electromagnetic radiation, shown as yellow lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:high-energy-event}, may occur at any stage of the process. Secondary semi-hard interactions between the proton remnants give rise to the \emph{underlying event}, represented by the purple blob in Fig.~\ref{fig:high-energy-event}. These interactions generate further radiation and hadrons, however are not described by standard QCD factorisation and require dedicated modelling. 

Prior to the hard interaction, the incoming partons may radiate, producing \emph{initial-state radiation} (ISR). This radiation evolves down to the hadronisation scale and contributes to the final-state hadrons (dark green blobs). The light blue ovals in Fig.~\ref{fig:high-energy-event} represent the beam remnants: the fragments of the incoming protons that do not participate in the hard scattering.

\subsection{The QCD Factorisation Formula}
\label{subsec:factorisation-formula}

To relate the partonic dynamics of a hard scattering to experimentally
measured hadronic cross sections, we employ the QCD factorisation
formula,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:factorisation-formula}
    d\sigma(P_1,P_2) = \sum_{i,j}\int_0^1 dx_1\,dx_2\,
    f_{i/P_1}(x_1,\mu_F^2)\,f_{j/P_2}(x_2,\mu_F^2)\,
    d\hat{\sigma}_{ij}\!\left(\alpha_s(\mu_R^2),\mu_R^2,\mu_F^2\right)
    + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}^2}{Q^2}\right),
\end{equation}
which is illustrated schematically in Fig.~\ref{fig:factorisation-formula}.
Here, $f_{i/P_1}(x_1,\mu_F^2)$ and $f_{j/P_2}(x_2,\mu_F^2)$ are the 
PDFs for partons $i$ and $j$ inside the incoming protons $P_1$ and 
$P_2$, carrying longitudinal momentum fractions $x_1$ and $x_2$,
The sum over $i,j$ runs over all contributing parton flavours, while 
$\hat{\sigma}_{ij}$ denotes the corresponding partonic cross section.

\section{Jet Physics}
\label{sec:jet-phys}

As discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:hadronisation}, jets
appear as highly collimated sprays of hadrons resulting
from the hadronisation of energetic quarks and gluons produced in
high-energy processes. They are among the most frequently
observed objects in proton--proton collisions at the LHC, and
are inherently complex and ambiguous objects. To be able to describe them in a
well-defined and reproducible way, a \emph{jet definition} is required, 
consisting of three components:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a \emph{jet algorithm}: a systematic procedure for clustering 
    final-state particles into jets, i.e.\ a set of rules that determine 
    which particles belong to the same jet;
    
    \item the algorithm's \emph{parameters}: these specify the proximity required between two particles for them to be recombined into a single jet constituent;
    
\item a \emph{recombination scheme}: this defines how the four-momenta of two particles are combined when they are merged. The most widely used is the $E$-scheme, which is a four-momentum sum, $p_i^\mu + p_j^\mu = p_k^\mu$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Jet algorithms}
\label{sec:jet-algs}

In 1990, significant discussion within the high-energy physics community 
led to the formulation of a set of criteria that a well-defined jet 
algorithm should satisfy. These requirements were outlined in a document 
known as the \emph{Snowmass Accord}~\cite{Huth:1990mi}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item simple to implement in an experimental analysis;
    \item simple to implement in a theoretical calculation;
    \item defined at any order in perturbation theory;
    \item yields a finite cross section at any order in perturbation theory;
    \item yields a cross section that is relatively insensitive to hadronisation effects.
\end{enumerate}
The final three requirements are ensured by IRC safety 
(Sec.~\ref{sec:IRC}), which guarantees that jet definitions at the 
detector, hadron, and parton levels are essentially equivalent.
For IRC-safe observables, the effects of hadronisation are suppressed by inverse powers of the energy scale $Q$ used in the process. Consequently, at higher energies, the correspondence between hadronic and partonic observables becomes increasingly accurate, consistent with quark--hadron duality (Sec.~\ref{sec:divergences}). Together, these requirements allow us to regard jets as well-defined objects whose properties can be either determined by their constituent hadrons or underlying partons. This enables direct comparison between experimental measurements and theoretical calculations at the parton level.

A wide variety of jet algorithms exist, each with its own advantages and limitations. The choice of algorithm depends on the specific physics question being addressed and the type of information one seeks to extract from an event. In general, jet algorithms can be divided into two broad classes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cone algorithms} cluster particles whose transverse
energy deposits fall within a fixed cone of radius $R$ in $(\eta,\phi)$ space. 
In three dimensions, these regions appear as cones. Different implementations vary in how stable 
cones are defined and identified, see Refs.~\cite{Salam:2007xv,Kidonakis:1998bk} 
for details. These are often described as \emph{top-down} algorithms.

    \item \textbf{Sequential recombination algorithms} iteratively merge the closest pair of particles according to a chosen distance measure, until a stopping criterion is reached. Variations arise from different definitions of the distance measure and stopping condition. These are often referred to as \emph{bottom-up} algorithms.
\end{itemize}
In this thesis, we focus exclusively on sequential recombination algorithms. For a comprehensive review of jet algorithms across different processes, see Refs.~\cite{Banfi:2016yyq, Salam:2010nqg}.

\section{Sequential recombination in $e^+e^-$ collisions}
\label{sec:ee-algos}

To illustrate the basic procedure of sequential recombination algorithms,
we first consider their application to $e^+e^-$ collisions. The JADE
algorithm, introduced by the \textsc{JADE} Collaboration
\cite{JADE:1988xlj}, was the first sequential recombination algorithm to
be used experimentally and proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Define a \emph{resolution parameter}, $y_{\mathrm{cut}}$.
    \item \label{step:jade-2} Compute the \emph{distance measure} for every pair of 
    particles $i$ and $j$:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:yij-jade}
        y_{ij} = \frac{2E_iE_j(1 - \cos\theta_{ij})}{Q^2}\,,
    \end{equation}
    where $Q$ is the total centre-of-mass energy, $E_i$ is the energy of particle $i$, and $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between particles $i$ and $j$.
    \item Identify the pair with the smallest $y_{ij}$. If $y_{ij} < y_{\mathrm{cut}}$, 
    recombine the two particles into a pseudojet according to the chosen recombination scheme.
    \item Repeat the procedure from step \ref{step:jade-2} until all remaining pairs satisfy $y_{ij} > y_{\mathrm{cut}}$. The surviving objects are defined as jets.
\end{enumerate}
A drawback of the JADE algorithm is its tendency to incorrectly cluster
soft partons that are widely separated in angle. This follows from the
energy dependence of the distance measure, $y_{ij}$, in Eq.~\eqref{eq:yij-jade},
which becomes small whenever either particle is soft, irrespective of
their angular separation. Consequently, two soft gluons emitted at widely
separated angles can be recombined before either is clustered with the
hard parton from which it originated. This can subsequently result in a
soft gluon being incorrectly associated with a different hard parton.

An example is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:JADE}. The soft gluons $k_1$ and
$k_2$ are first recombined into the pseudojet $k_1+k_2$, which then
clusters with the hard parton $p_1$. As a result, the radiation $k_2$ is
associated with $p_1$, despite being emitted from the other hard parton.
This behaviour is undesirable, since one would generally expect a jet to
consist of a hard parton together with its associated radiation.

\section{Jet algorithms beyond $e^+e^-$}
\label{sec:beyond-ee}

The clustering procedure becomes more involved when moving beyond
$e^+e^-$ collisions to processes involving incoming hadrons, such as
hadronic collisions and deep-inelastic scattering (DIS). In these cases,
the algorithm must also account for radiation that becomes collinear to an
incoming beam. This is incorporated through an additional \emph{beam
distance}, $d_{iB}$, which determines whether a particle is associated
with the beam or recombined with another particle.

The construction of jet algorithms for DIS is particularly interesting
because the presence of a single hadronic beam introduces an asymmetry that
is absent in both $e^+e^-$ and hadronic collisions. This means that the
radiation associated with the proton remnant and the struck quark must be
treated carefully within the jet definition. The next chapter discusses
this in detail. We first introduce the relevant DIS kinematics and the
Breit frame, before reviewing the development of DIS jet algorithms and
more recent proposals. We then introduce the inclusive generalised-$k_t$
algorithm for DIS and investigate its properties.
