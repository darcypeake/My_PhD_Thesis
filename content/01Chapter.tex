\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) is, as its name suggests, a particle accelerator
designed to collide hadrons at energies far beyond those achieved by previous
experiments. Having recently concluded its third run, it has spent over a decade
probing physics at the fundamental scale. The Standard Model (SM) is currently our
best theory for describing the interactions between elementary particles. It
describes the electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions, with the latter
taking centre stage throughout this thesis, titled \emph{Jet Physics at the LHC
and Beyond}. The strong interaction, described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD),
plays a central role in almost every measurement made at the LHC. Whether in
measurements of the Higgs boson, studies of the top quark, or searches for physics
beyond the Standard Model, hard-scattering processes are accompanied by QCD
radiation, giving rise to the complex hadronic final states observed in
experiments. Reliable theoretical predictions for this radiation are therefore
essential for interpreting LHC data and extracting the underlying physics.

Like other quantum field theories, QCD exhibits behaviour that depends on the
energy scale at which it is probed. At high energies, the interaction between
quarks and gluons becomes weak, allowing them to behave approximately as free
particles. This property, known as asymptotic freedom, permits the use of
perturbation theory to obtain systematic theoretical predictions. At low energies,
however, the strong interaction becomes increasingly strong, causing quarks and
gluons to bind together into hadrons. In this regime, perturbation theory is no
longer applicable and non-perturbative techniques are required. Throughout this
thesis we focus on the high-energy regime, where perturbative QCD has proven
remarkably successful and provides the foundation for precision phenomenology at
the LHC.

Despite the remarkable success of perturbative QCD, obtaining precise theoretical
predictions presents a number of challenges. This thesis addresses two such
problems, concerning the definition of jets and the treatment of large logarithmic
corrections.

The first concerns the fact that the quarks and gluons produced in a hard scattering
are never observed directly. Instead, they undergo further QCD radiation and
hadronisation, producing collimated sprays of hadrons known as \emph{jets}. 
A jet is an inherently ambiguous object, and defining one requires a prescription for clustering final-state particles
-- whether partons in a theoretical calculation or energy deposits in a detector --
into jets in a way that can be applied consistently to both. This prescription is
known as a \emph{jet algorithm}. Different clustering procedures can lead to different jet
configurations, and the choice of algorithm can therefore have a significant impact
on the resulting predictions and on the physical information that can be extracted.
Designing an algorithm suited to a particular process or observable is consequently
a non-trivial task, requiring careful consideration of both the underlying kinematics
and the properties that the resulting jets should possess. This is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS}, where we introduce a new inclusive generalised-$k_t$ jet
algorithm for deep-inelastic scattering (DIS).

The second challenge arises from the appearance of \emph{large logarithms} in
perturbative calculations. For many observables, logarithmically enhanced
contributions arise in regions of phase space where the observable becomes small.
These logarithms can become sufficently large that the terms within the perturbative expansion
no longer converge, thereby reducing the reliability of fixed-order
predictions. Accurate theoretical predictions in these regions therefore require these logarithms to be
summed to all orders in perturbation theory through a procedure known as
\emph{resummation}. As with fixed-order calculations, resummation can be performed at
successively higher levels of logarithmic accuracy. Several approaches have been developed to
perform such resummations. In this thesis, we focus on the semi-numerical
\textsc{CAESAR} and \textsc{ARES} formalisms. In particular, Chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}
extends the \textsc{ARES} framework to hadronic collisions, incorporating the
effects of initial-state radiation at next-to-next-to-leading logarithmic (NNLL)
accuracy for the first time, through the process $hh \to Z + \text{jet}$.

The original work presented in this thesis addresses each of these problems in turn.
Chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS} introduces a new inclusive generalised-$k_t$ jet algorithm
for DIS, formulated in the Breit frame, together with an infrared and collinear safe
prescription for identifying the jet containing the struck quark, and a
phenomenological study of its behaviour and its sensitivity to non-perturbative
effects. The algorithm is implemented as a publicly available plugin to
\textsc{FastJet}, and is presented in Ref.~\cite{vanBeekveld:2026rez}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet} completes the crucial conceptual stages of extending the
\textsc{ARES} formalism to hadronic collisions with initial-state radiation at
next-to-next-to-leading logarithmic accuracy, which has not been done before, and
applies it to the process $hh \to Z + \text{jet}$. The results have been validated
through a combination of direct and indirect checks, all of which are successful.

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:QCD} introduces
the background material on QCD necessary to understand the remaining parts of this
thesis, where we introduce notation and relevant ideas used in the original research.
Here we give an overview of perturbation theory, the running of the strong coupling,
and illustrate theoretically what occurs in a high-energy collision. Chapter~\ref{ch:resum} turns to resummation, introducing the
formalism and background required for chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}, where the \textsc{ARES}
framework is extended to hadronic initial states and applied to
$hh \to Z + \text{jet}$ at next-to-next-to-leading logarithmic accuracy. Finally,
chapter~\ref{ch:conc} concludes, discussing how the research presented here could be
extended and its implications for the wider community.
