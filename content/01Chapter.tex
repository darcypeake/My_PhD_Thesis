\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) is, as its name suggests, a particle accelerator that
collides hadrons at energies far beyond those achieved by any previous experiment. Now
having concluded its third run, it continues to probe physics at the fundamental scale. The Standard
Model (SM) is currently the best theory we have to describe the interactions between
the elementary particles and their behaviours. It describes the electromagnetic, weak
and strong interactions, with the latter taking centre stage throughout the course of
this thesis, titled \emph{Jet physics at the LHC and beyond}. The strong force, also
known as quantum chromodynamics (QCD), sits at the heart of almost every measurement
made at the LHC. Whether that be the determination of the Higgs boson, top quarks, or
searches for physics beyond the Standard Model, the events observed at the LHC are
overwhelmed by QCD radiation, such that reliable theoretical predictions are necessary
to understand this.

Like other quantum field theories, QCD exhibits behaviour that depends on the energy
scale at which it is probed. At high energies, the interaction between the particles of
the strong force, namely the quarks and gluons, is weak, and the particles act freely.
In this regime one can use perturbation theory to model QCD. At low energies, on the
other hand, the interaction between the quarks and gluons becomes increasingly strong,
such that the particles cluster together. As a result, particles can no longer act
freely, and perturbation theory cannot be used. In this thesis we restrict ourselves
to the high-energy regime, where perturbation theory has proven remarkably
successful and underlies every precision QCD prediction made for the LHC.

Even within perturbation theory, however, there remain problems one faces in QCD. 
In the scope of this thesis, we focus on two problems, specifically those which
complicate the comparison between theory and experiment. The first arises from the fact
that, in a high-energy collision, the quarks and gluons produced in a hard scattering
undergo a process known as hadronisation, such that they evolve into collimated sprays
of colourless hadrons known as \emph{jets}. A jet is a messy and ambiguous object and
defining one requires a prescription for clustering final-state particles -- whether
partons in a theoretical calculation or energy deposits in a detector -- into jets in a
way that can be applied consistently to both. This prescription is known as a \emph{jet
algorithm}. As one might suspect, different clustering procedures lead to different
jets, and so choosing and designing an algorithm suited to a given process and
observable is a non-trivial task in its own right, requiring careful thought about what
physics we are trying to extract and which questions we want to answer. This is the
subject of chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS}, where a new jet algorithm for deep-inelastic
scattering, i.e.\ electron-proton collisions, is presented. 

The second problem is the presence of \emph{large logarithms} within perturbation
theory. These occur for certain observables in phase-space regions where the observable
becomes small. This causes a mismatch between perturbation theory and experimental data in 
these regions. In this sense perturbation theory alone cannot be used to obtain reliable predictions
in this region and so it is necessary for us to perform something known as \emph{resummation}
to tackle these large logarithms. Resummation can be performed to various accuracies the 
way in which perturbation theory can. Various methods exist in the literature to perform this resummation;
however, for the scope of this thesis we focus on semi-numerical formalisms, particularly
those known as \textsc{CAESAR} and \textsc{ARES}. This is the subject of
chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}, where the semi-numerical \textsc{ARES} resummation formalism
is extended to initial-state radiation for the first time, with the process
$hh \to Z + \text{jet}$ as its application.

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:QCD}
introduces the background material on QCD necessary to understand the remaining
parts of this thesis where we introduce notation and relevant ideas used in the original research.
Here we will give an overview of perturbation theory, the running of the strong coupling, and 
the illustrating theoretically what occurs in a high-energy collision.  
Chapter~\ref{ch:jet-phys} provides us with a brief interlude where it discusses 
what a jet is, how to define them, algorithms and the discussions of advancements in
jet algorithms, setting up chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS}, which
presents the new deep inelastic scattering jet algorithm mentioned above. Chapter~\ref{ch:resum}
turns to resummation, introducing the formalism and background required for
chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}, where the \textsc{ARES} framework is extended to hadronic
initial states and applied to $hh \to Z + \text{jet}$ at next-to-next-to-leading
logarithmic accuracy. Finally, chapter~\ref{ch:conc} concludes, discussing how the
research presented here could be extended and its implications for the wider
community.
