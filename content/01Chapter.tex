\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) is, as its name suggests, a particle accelerator that
collides hadrons at energies far beyond those achieved by any previous experiment.
Having recently concluded its third run, it has spent over a decade probing physics at
the fundamental scale. The Standard Model (SM) is currently the best theory we have to
describe the interactions between the elementary particles and their behaviours. It
describes the electromagnetic, weak and strong interactions, with the latter taking
centre stage throughout the course of this thesis, titled \emph{Jet physics at the LHC
and beyond}. The strong force, also known as quantum chromodynamics (QCD), sits at the
heart of almost every measurement made at the LHC. Whether that be the determination of
the Higgs boson, top quarks, or searches for physics beyond the Standard Model, the
events observed at the LHC are overwhelmed by QCD radiation, such that reliable
theoretical predictions are necessary to interpret them.

Like other quantum field theories, QCD exhibits behaviour that depends on the energy
scale at which it is probed. At high energies, the interaction between quarks and
gluons becomes weak, allowing them to behave approximately as free particles. This
property, known as asymptotic freedom, permits the use of perturbation theory to obtain
systematic theoretical predictions. At low energies, however, the strong interaction
becomes stronger, causing quarks and gluons to bind together into hadrons. In this
regime perturbation theory is no longer applicable and requires other theoretical techniques.
Throughout this thesis we focus exclusively on the high-energy regime, where perturbative QCD has proven remarkably successful and provides the foundation for precision phenomenology at the LHC.  

Despite this success, perturbative QCD presents a number of challenges when comparing
theoretical predictions with experimental measurements. This thesis addresses two such
problems.

The first concerns the fact that the quarks and gluons produced in a hard scattering
are never observed directly. Instead, they undergo hadronisation, evolving into
collimated sprays of hadrons known as \emph{jets}. A jet is a messy and ambiguous object and
defining one requires a prescription for clustering final-state particles -- whether
partons in a theoretical calculation or energy deposits in a detector -- into jets in a
way that can be applied consistently to both. This prescription is known as a \emph{jet
algorithm}. Different clustering procedures generally lead to different
jet configurations, and so choosing and designing an algorithm suited to a given process and
observable is a non-trivial task in its own right, requiring careful thought about what
physics we are trying to extract and which questions we want to answer. This is the
subject of chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS}, where a new jet algorithm for deep-inelastic
scattering, i.e.\ electron-proton collisions, is presented.

The second challenge arises from the appearance of \emph{large logarithms} in
perturbative calculations. For many observables, these logarithmic contributions become
enhanced in regions of phase space where the observable takes small values, causing the
perturbative expansion to converge poorly and reducing the reliability of fixed-order
predictions. Accurate theoretical predictions therefore require these logarithms to be
summed to all orders in perturbation theory through a procedure known as
\emph{resummation}. As with fixed-order calculations, resummation can be performed at
successively higher levels of logarithmic accuracy. Several approaches have been
developed to achieve this, and in this thesis we focus on the semi-numerical
\textsc{CAESAR} and \textsc{ARES} formalisms. In particular, Chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}
extends the \textsc{ARES} framework to include initial-state radiation for the first
time, using the process $hh \to Z + \text{jet}$ as its primary application.

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:QCD} introduces
the background material on QCD necessary to understand the remaining parts of this
thesis, where we introduce notation and relevant ideas used in the original research.
Here we give an overview of perturbation theory, the running of the strong coupling,
and illustrate theoretically what occurs in a high-energy collision.
Chapter~\ref{ch:jet-phys} provides a brief interlude discussing what a jet is, how jets
are defined, and recent advancements in jet algorithms, setting up
chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS}, which presents the new deep-inelastic-scattering jet
algorithm mentioned above. Chapter~\ref{ch:resum} turns to resummation, introducing the
formalism and background required for chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}, where the \textsc{ARES}
framework is extended to hadronic initial states and applied to
$hh \to Z + \text{jet}$ at next-to-next-to-leading logarithmic accuracy. Finally,
chapter~\ref{ch:conc} concludes, discussing how the research presented here could be
extended and its implications for the wider community.
