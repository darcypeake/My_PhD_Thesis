The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) is, as its name suggests, a particle accelerator that collides
hadrons at energies far beyond those achieved by any previous experiment. Now in its third run, it continues to
probe physics of the fundamental scale. The standard model (SM) is currently the best theory we have
to describe the interactions between the elementary particles and their behaviours. It describes
the electromagnetic, weak and strong interactions, with the latter taking centre stage throughout the course
of this thesis, titled \emph{jet physics at the LHC and beyond}. The strong force, also known as 
quantum chromodynamics (QCD) sits at the heart of almost every measurement made at the LHC.
Whether that be the determination of the Higgs boson, top quarks, or searches for physics beyond the Standard Model, the events observed at the LHC are overwhelmed by QCD radiation such
that reliable theoretical predictions are necessary to understand this.

QCD, like other quantum field theories, exhibits behaviour that depends strongly on
the energy scale at which it is probed. At low energies the particles of the strong force, 
quarks and gluons are confined, binding together into the hadrons that are the final-state particles
observed in collider detectors. The interaction is strong and cannot be treated
by perturbation theory. At high energies, by contrast, asymptotic freedom means that quarks
and gluons behave approximately as free particles, and the coupling becomes small
enough that perturbation theory -- an expansion in powers of the strong coupling
$\alpha_s$ -- is applicable. This perturbative regime has proven remarkably successful:
it underlies essentially every precision QCD prediction made for the LHC. This thesis
focuses exclusively on this high-energy, perturbative regime.

Even within perturbation theory, however, two related problems complicate the
comparison between theory and experiment. The first is that quarks and gluons
produced in a hard scattering are not themselves observed instead, through the
process of hadronisation, they evolve into collimated sprays of colourless hadrons
known as \emph{jets}. A jet is not an object defined by the underlying theory but by
an algorithm -- a prescription for clustering final-state particles, whether partons
in a theoretical calculation or energy deposits in a detector, into jets in a way that
can be applied consistently to both. Different clustering procedures lead to
different jets, with different sensitivities to soft and collinear radiation, and
choosing or designing an algorithm suited to a given process and observable is a
non-trivial theoretical problem in its own right. This is the subject of
chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS}, where a new jet algorithm for deep-inelastic scattering is
presented.

The second problem is that of \emph{large logarithms}. Fixed-order perturbation
theory computes an observable as a power series in $\alpha_s$, with each order
receiving corrections from an increasing number of real and virtual emissions. When
an observable is inclusive, these corrections are typically well-behaved. However,
many observables of interest are sensitive to soft and collinear radiation, and in
the phase-space regions where such an observable becomes small, each order of the
perturbative expansion contains logarithms of that observable that grow large enough
to compensate the smallness of $\alpha_s$. The fixed-order expansion then breaks
down, and an infinite tower of these logarithmically enhanced terms must instead be
summed to all orders -- a technique known as \emph{resummation} -- in order to
restore a reliable, convergent prediction. This is the second problem addressed in
this thesis, and is the subject of chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}, where the semi-numerical
\textsc{ARES} resummation formalism is extended to initial-state radiation for the
first time, with the process $pp \to Z + \text{jet}$ as its application.

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:QCD}
introduces the background material on QCD needed to understand the original research
presented later, including the running of the coupling, factorisation, and parton
evolution. Chapter~\ref{ch:jet-phys} gives a brief overview of jets and the
considerations that go into defining them, setting up chapter~\ref{chapter:DIS}, which
presents the new deep inelastic scattering jet algorithm mentioned above. Chapter~\ref{ch:resum}
turns to resummation, introducing the formalism and background required for
chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}, where the \textsc{ARES} framework is extended to hadronic
initial states and applied to $pp \to Z + \text{jet}$ at next-to-next-to-leading
logarithmic accuracy. Finally, chapter~\ref{ch:conc} concludes, discussing how the
research presented here could be extended and its implications for the wider
community.
