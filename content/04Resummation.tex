\chapter{Resummation}
\label{ch:resum}

In this chapter, we discuss \emph{resummation}. As 
outlined in Sec.~\ref{subsec:QCD-ev}, following the initial hard 
scattering, the coloured partons produced undergo QCD radiation dominated 
by soft and collinear emissions. For many observables, these emissions 
give rise to logarithmically enhanced contributions, which become large in 
the limit where the observable, $v$, is small. In this regime, fixed-order 
perturbation theory becomes unreliable and resummation is required to obtain meaningful predictions. 

Traditionally, resummation for final-state observables was performed in an
observable-dependent manner, requiring separate analytical calculations for
each case~\cite{Collins:1984kg, Catani:1992ua, Bonciani:2003nt}. A first
step towards a more general approach was taken in~\cite{Banfi:2001bz},
where a systematic semi-numerical framework was developed to achieve NLL
accuracy for a broad class of observables. This framework was subsequently
extended and implemented in the computer code \textsc{CAESAR}
(\emph{Computer Automated Expert Semi-Analytical Resummer})~\cite{Banfi:2004yd}.
In this approach, the resummation formula is expressed in terms of a set of
observable-dependent parameters, which can be determined automatically and
numerically for a given observable. The framework has been used to resum a
broad class of observables, including event shapes and jet rates. This
approach was subsequently extended in \textsc{ARES}
(\emph{Automated Resummation for Event Shapes})~\cite{Banfi:2014sua,
Banfi:2016zlc, Banfi:2018mcq, Arpino:2019ozn}, which achieves NNLL
accuracy for jet observables in $e^+e^-$ collisions. In this thesis, we
work within the \textsc{ARES} framework.

The structure of this chapter is as follows. We begin by introducing event-shape
observables and the properties they must satisfy. We then motivate the need
for resummation through a fixed-order calculation of a standard event shape,
thrust, making explicit the origin and structure of the large logarithms.
Finally, we present the NLL resummation formula and the key ingredients
required to evaluate it for a given observable. This chapter provides the
necessary foundation for the original research presented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:hhZjet}.

\section{Event shapes}
\label{sec:event-shapes}

Event shapes are IRC safe observables constructed 
from the four-momenta of final-state particles. They characterise the geometry 
of an event by quantifying the distribution of energy-momentum flow in the 
hadronic final state. Unlike jet algorithms (see Sec.~\ref{sec:jet-algs}), which cluster particles into 
discrete jets, event shapes provide a continuous description of the 
event geometry, making them powerful probes of QCD 
radiation. They have been widely used in precision studies of QCD, most 
notably in the extraction of the strong coupling constant 
$\alpha_s$~\cite{Becher:2008cf, Abbate:2010xh}. Notable examples 
include:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Thrust}~\cite{Farhi:1977sg, Brandt:1964sa} is one of the
most widely studied event-shape observables. It is defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:thrust}
	T \equiv \max_{\vec{n}} \frac{\sum_i |\vec{p}_i \cdot \vec{n}|}{\sum_i |\vec{p}_i|}\,, \quad \tau \equiv 1 - T \,,
\end{equation}
where $\vec{p}_i$ denotes the three-momentum of particle $i$ in the final state. 
The unit vector $\vec{n}$ that maximises this quantity is referred to as the \emph{thrust axis}, $\vec{n}_T$. 
This axis separates the event into two distinct hemispheres, $\mathcal{H}_1$ and $\mathcal{H}_2$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:thrust}. 
In practice, one usually works with the variable $\tau$, as defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:thrust}.
For $e^+e^- \to \mathrm{hadrons}$, thrust provides a measure of how ``pencil-like'' an event is: $\tau \to 0$ corresponds to the Born-level configuration with a perfectly back-to-back $q\bar{q}$ pair, while larger values of $\tau$ indicate more spherically distributed multi-particle final state.

\item \textbf{Jet broadenings}~\cite{Clavelli:1979md, Rakow:1981qn, 
Catani:1992jc} quantify the transverse momentum of final-state hadrons 
relative to the thrust axis. The event is divided into two hemispheres 
$\mathcal{H}_1$ and $\mathcal{H}_2$, and for each hemisphere the 
broadening is defined as

\begin{equation}
	B_\ell \equiv \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{H}_\ell} |\vec{p}_i \times \vec{n}_T|}{2 \sum_i |\vec{p}_i|}, \quad \ell = 1, 2\,.
\end{equation}
The wide-jet broadening, narrow-jet broadening, and total 
broadening are defined respectively as  
\begin{equation}
    B_W \equiv \max\{B_1, B_2\}\,, \qquad 
    B_N \equiv \min\{B_1, B_2\}\,, \qquad 
    B_T \equiv B_1 + B_2\,.
\end{equation}

\item{\textbf{Jet masses}}~\cite{Chandramohan:1980ry, Clavelli:1981yh} are defined by computing the invariant mass of each hemisphere,
\begin{equation}
	\rho_{\ell}\equiv \frac{\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}p_i\right)^2}{\left(\sum_iE_i\right)^2}\,,\quad \ell=1, 2\,,
\end{equation}
where $E_i$ is the energy of the particle $i$ in the final-state.
The heavy-jet mass and light-jet mass are defined respectively as
\begin{equation}
	\rho_H\equiv\max\left\{\rho_1,\rho_2\right\}\,,\quad \rho_L\equiv\min\left\{\rho_1,\rho_2\right\}\,.
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

While event shapes offer detailed insight into the structure of hadronic 
final states, their perturbative predictions are complicated by large 
logarithmic contributions arising from soft and collinear emissions in 
the limit where the observable approaches zero. To illustrate this 
explicitly, we now examine how such logarithms emerge in the fixed-order 
calculation of thrust.

\section{Motivation: thrust and the need for resummation}
\label{sec:thrust-example}

To illustrate the need for resummation, we consider a fixed-order 
calculation of the thrust distribution. At Born level, the process 
$e^+e^- \to q\bar{q}$ produces a perfectly back-to-back quark--antiquark 
pair with $\tau = 0$, giving the distribution
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\sigma_0}\frac{d\sigma}{d\tau} = \delta(\tau),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_0$ is the leading-order cross section 
defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma-LO}.The first non-trivial contribution arises 
at $\mathcal{O}(\alpha_s)$ from the real-emission process $
e^+e^- \to q\bar{q}g$. For a 
three-particle final state with energy fractions satisfying 
$x_q + x_{\bar{q}} + x_g = 2$, the thrust distribution in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:thrust} reduces to
\begin{equation}
T = \max[x_q, x_{\bar{q}}, x_g]\,,
\end{equation}
with $T_{\min} = 2/3$ for the symmetric
configuration $x_q = x_{\bar{q}} = x_g = 2/3$, and $T_{\max} = 1$.

Using the real-emission cross section in Eq.~\eqref{eq:qqg-real}, the
differential thrust distribution is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\sigma_0}\frac{d\sigma}{dT}
    = C_F \frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}
      \int_0^1 dx_q \int_0^1 dx_{\bar{q}}\,
      \frac{x_q^2 + x_{\bar{q}}^2}{(1-x_q)(1-x_{\bar{q}})}
      \, \Theta\!\left(x_q+x_{\bar{q}}-1\right)
      \delta\!\left(T - \max[x_q, x_{\bar{q}}, x_g]\right)\,.
\end{equation}
As discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:IR-div}, this expression
contains soft and collinear singularities. Regulating these singularities
using dimensional regularisation gives
\begin{multline}
    \frac{1}{\sigma_0}\frac{d\sigma}{dT}
    = C_F\frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}
      \left(\frac{4\pi\mu_R^2}{s}\right)^\varepsilon
      \frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\varepsilon)}
      \int_0^1 dx_q \int_0^1 dx_{\bar{q}} \\
    \times
      \frac{(x_q^2+x_{\bar{q}}^2)
            -\varepsilon(2-x_q-x_{\bar{q}})^2}
           {(1-x_q)^{1+\varepsilon}\,
            (1-x_{\bar{q}})^{1+\varepsilon}\,
            (x_q+x_{\bar{q}}-1)^\varepsilon}
      \,\Theta\!\left(x_q+x_{\bar{q}}-1\right)
      \delta\!\left(T - \max[x_q, x_{\bar{q}}, x_g]\right)\,.
\end{multline}
The resulting phase-space integrals contain poles in $\varepsilon$, which
cancel against the corresponding poles in the virtual contribution.
Since the virtual contribution is proportional to $\delta(\tau)$, its
finite coefficient can be fixed by requiring that the complete
distribution reproduces the total cross section at NLO, given in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma-full}. Changing variables to
$\tau\equiv1-T$, this gives the complete thrust distribution at
$\mathcal{O}(\alpha_s)$,

        \left( \frac{\ln \tau}{\tau} \right)_+
    \right\}\,.

The plus distributions are defined by
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^1 d\tau
    \left[\frac{\ln^k\tau}{\tau}\right]_{\!+}
    g(\tau)
    \equiv
    \int_0^1 d\tau\,\frac{\ln^k\tau}{\tau}
    \left[g(\tau)-g(0)\right]\,,
\end{equation}
so that for $\tau > 0$ they reduce to the ordinary functions
$\ln^k\tau/\tau$. 
In the two-jet limit, $\tau\to0$, the fixed-order distribution contains
logarithmically enhanced contributions that become increasingly large.
Keeping only these terms, Eq.~\eqref{eq:full-thrust-NLO} gives
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{1}{\sigma_0}\frac{d\sigma}{d\tau}\right)_{\!\text{log-enh}}
    = C_F \frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi}
      \left[
        -3 \left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)_+
        - 4 \left(\frac{\ln \tau}{\tau}\right)_+
      \right]\,.
\end{equation}
It is useful to consider the cumulative distribution, or cumulant,
defined by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cumulant}
    \Sigma(\tau)
    = \frac{1}{\sigma_0}\int_0^\tau d\tau'\, \frac{d\sigma}{d\tau'}\,.
\end{equation}
Using 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:plus-dist-integrals}
    \int_0^\tau d\tau'\,\left(\frac{1}{\tau'}\right)_+ = -L\,, 
    \qquad
    \int_0^\tau d\tau'\,\left(\frac{\ln\tau'}{\tau'}\right)_+ 
    = \frac{L^2}{2}\,,
\end{equation}
where $L \equiv \ln(1/\tau)$, the logarithmically enhanced contribution to the cumulant is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:thrust-cumulant}
    \Sigma_{\text{log-enh}}(\tau)
    = C_F \frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi} \left( -2L^2 + 3L \right)\,.
\end{equation}
All remaining terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:full-thrust-NLO} remain finite as 
$\tau \to 0$ and do not contribute logarithmic enhancements. The 
logarithmic contributions therefore dominate the behaviour of the fixed-order
prediction in the two-jet limit, where $\tau \to 0$ corresponds to
an exactly back-to-back $q\bar{q}$ configuration. At higher
orders, the cumulant receives contributions of the form
$\alpha_s^n L^m$ with $m\leq 2n$, since each power of $\alpha_s$ can
generate up to two logarithms from the soft and collinear divergences. As
$\tau\to0$, these logarithms become increasingly large and spoil the
convergence of the fixed-order expansion.

This behaviour is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:thrust-data-FO}. While 
the experimental data exhibits a turning point and local maximum in the two-jet region, 
the fixed-order predictions continue to increase as $\tau\to0$. 
Including higher-order corrections, moving from LO to NLO to NNLO, does not
resolve this behaviour, since the same logarithmically enhanced contributions appear at every order
in perturbation theory. Therefore, fixed-order predictions are not
sufficient to describe the two-jet region, and a resummation of these
logarithmically enhanced contributions is required.

To restore predictive power in the region $\tau \ll 1$, the perturbative 
expansion must be reorganised. When $\alpha_s L \sim 1$, successive 
terms in the perturbative series are no longer suppressed, so an 
expansion in powers of $\alpha_s$ alone is insufficient. Instead, one 
must perform a systematic \emph{resummation} of the logarithmically enhanced 
contributions to all orders in $\alpha_s$. In this region, the cumulant can 
therefore be reorganised as 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:resummed-cumulant}
	\Sigma(\tau) \simeq 
	\exp \left\{ 
	L g_1(\alpha_s L) 
	+ g_2(\alpha_s L) 
	+ \alpha_s g_3(\alpha_s L) 
	+ \cdots 
	\right\}\,.
\end{equation}

It is worth emphasising the distinction between fixed-order and logarithmic
accuracy. In a fixed-order calculation, improving the accuracy means
computing additional Feynman diagrams at one higher power of $\alpha_s$.
By contrast, each level of logarithmic accuracy already encodes an infinite
set of diagrams --- those contributing at the corresponding logarithmic
order at every power of $\alpha_s$. Advancing from one logarithmic order to the next therefore 
requires identifying which physical effects contribute at that order and 
computing each of their contributions at the necessary accuracy.

Resummation restores predictive power in the two-jet region by
systematically accounting for the logarithmically enhanced contributions
to all orders in perturbation theory. The resulting Sudakov suppression
at small $\tau$, together with the fixed-order behaviour away from the
two-jet limit, gives rise to a maximum in the distribution known as the
\emph{Sudakov peak}. This suppression is encoded in the Sudakov form
factor, which represents the probability of emitting no radiation
above the scale $\tau$. Within the \textsc{ARES} formalism, the Sudakov
form factor is described by the \emph{radiator}, which will be introduced
in Sec.~\ref{sec:NLL-resum}.

The appearance of large logarithms and the need for resummation are not
unique to thrust. The structure of Eq.~\eqref{eq:resummed-cumulant} applies
broadly to all IRC safe event-shape observables. The semi-numerical 
framework, implemented in the codes \textsc{CAESAR} and \textsc{ARES}, 
provides an observable-independent method for performing this resummation 
systematically. This will be discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:NLL-resum}.

\section{Problem specification}
\label{sec:problem-spec}

Having motivated the need for resummation using the thrust distribution,
we now formulate the general problem. We consider events with $n_i$ incoming partons and an arbitrary number $n$ of
final-state particles, whose momenta are denoted $p_1,\dots,p_n$.
At Born level the process involves $N$ hard partons in total -- including
both the $n_i$ incoming legs and the $N - n_i$ outgoing legs -- which define
the underlying hard configuration. We define an observable $V(p_1,\dots,p_n)$ as a non-negative function of
the final-state momenta. We assume the observable to be IRC safe and to vanish
smoothly as the event approaches an $N$-parton configuration.
Such observables are commonly referred to as $(N\!+\!1)$-jet observables,
where both the outgoing jets and the incoming beam jets are included in the
jet counting.

To select events containing $N$ or more well-separated hard jets, we 
introduce a selection function $\mathcal{H}(p_1,\dots,p_n)$, defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:H-def}
\mathcal{H}(p_1,\dots,p_n)=
\begin{cases}
1\,, & \text{if the event satisfies the selection criteria,}\\
0\,, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
This can be implemented, for instance, through a jet algorithm that counts 
the number of jets in the event, or by imposing a cut on a suitable 
$N$-jet observable. The total $N$-jet cross section for selected events is 
then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sigma-H}
  \sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
  = \sum_{n=N-n_i}^{\infty}
    \int d\Phi_n\,\frac{d\sigma_n}{d\Phi_n}\,
    \mathcal{H}(p_1,\dots,p_n)\,,
\end{equation}
where $d\sigma_n/d\Phi_n$ is the differential cross section for producing
$n$ final-state particles and $d\Phi_n$ is the corresponding $n$-particle
phase space, defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:m-3-phase-space}. The sum begins at
$n = N - n_i$, the minimum number of final-state particles required to
form the $N$-parton Born configuration.

We are particularly interested in the cumulative distribution of the 
observable $V$ for selected events in which $V$ takes a value smaller 
than $v$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Sigma-H}
  \Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(v)
  = \frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}}
    \sum_{n=N-n_i}^{\infty}
    \int d\Phi_n\,\frac{d\sigma_n}{d\Phi_n}\,
    \Theta\!\left(v - V(p_1,\dots,p_n)\right)
    \mathcal{H}(p_1,\dots,p_n)\,.
\end{equation}
In the limit $v\ll1$, where logarithmically enhanced contributions become
important, the cumulant $\Sigma_{\mathcal H}(v)$ assumes the factorised
form~\cite{Banfi:2004yd}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Sigma-H-fact}
  \Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(v)
  \simeq
  \frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}}
  \int d\Phi_N\,\frac{d\sigma_N}{d\Phi_N}\,
  \Sigma\!\left(\{p\},v\right)
  \mathcal{H}(p_{n_i+1},\dots,p_N)\,,
\end{equation}
where $\{p\} \equiv \{p_1,\dots,p_N\}$ denotes the full set of $N$ Born
momenta, with $p_1,\dots,p_{n_i}$ the incoming legs and
$p_{n_i+1},\dots,p_N$ the outgoing legs. The term $d\sigma_N/d\Phi_N$ is the
leading-order cross section for the Born configuration, and the integral is performed
over the $N$-particle Born phase space $d\Phi_N$. 
The factorisation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sigma-H-fact} separates the Born kinematics, encoded in
$d\sigma_N/d\Phi_N$ and $\mathcal{H}$, from the effects of soft and
collinear radiation, which are captured entirely by $\Sigma(\{p\},v)$.
In the limit $v\ll1$, the latter contains the
logarithmically enhanced contributions that lead to the breakdown of the
fixed-order expansion discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:thrust-example}. The
remainder of this chapter is devoted to their resummation.

\section{Observable requirements}
\label{sec:obs-req}

Before deriving the resummation formula, it is necessary to specify the
class of observables for which the \textsc{CAESAR} and \textsc{ARES}
formalism applies. Beyond IRC safety, these observables must satisfy a
number of additional requirements. Specifically, for
$V(p_1,p_2,\dots)$ these conditions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Vanishes smoothly in the soft-collinear limit}: the observable
must vanish continuously as a single additional emission of momentum $k$
becomes soft and collinear to a hard leg $\ell$. In this limit, the observable
can be parametrised as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:general-sc-V}
    V(\{\tilde{p}\},k) \simeq V_{\text{sc}}(\{\tilde{p}\},k) 
    \equiv d_\ell\left(\frac{k_t^{(\ell)}}{Q}\right)^a 
    e^{-b_\ell\eta^{(\ell)}} g_\ell(\phi^{(\ell)})\,,
\end{equation}
where $V_{\text{sc}}$ denotes the observable evaluated in the 
soft-collinear limit. Here, $\{\tilde{p}\}$ represents the Born momenta
after recoil from the emission $k$, while
$k_t^{(\ell)}$, $\eta^{(\ell)}$, and $\phi^{(\ell)}$ denote the transverse
momentum, rapidity, and azimuthal angle of $k$ with respect to the emitting
leg $p_\ell$, respectively. The scale $Q$ is an arbitrary hard scale of the process, while 
the parameters $a$,  $b_\ell$, $d_\ell$, and the function $g_\ell(\phi)$ characterise the 
observable in this limit. These quantities
can be determined for each emitting leg and form the basic ingredients
of the resummation formula. IRC safety requires $a > 0$ and 
$b_\ell > -a$. \ABquestion{The scale $Q$ is a hard scale but in hadronic collisions
it is a resummation scale. Does the hard and resummation scale coincide for $\ee$?}

\item \textbf{Recursively IRC (rIRC) safe}: rIRC safety requires that
the observable is insensitive to the addition of an emission that is
relatively much softer and/or more collinear than the existing ensemble of
soft and collinear emissions. A formal definition is given in Refs.~\cite{Banfi:2004yd, Banfi:2003je}. The key requirements are summarised below.

Consider momenta $\kappa_i(\zeta_i)$ satisfying 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rIRCa}
	V(\{\tilde{p}\},\kappa_i(\zeta_i))=\zeta_i\,,
\end{equation}
where in the soft and/or collinear limits $\zeta_i\to0$, the azimuthal angle $\phi_i$ of each emission $\kappa_i(\zeta_i)$ is held fixed. The functions $\kappa_i(\zeta_i)$ may differ for each emission but must satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:rIRCa}.
The collinear splitting of an existing emission is denoted 
\begin{equation}
	\kappa_i(\zeta)\to\{\kappa_{i_a},\kappa_{i_b}\}(\zeta,\mu)\,,
\end{equation}
where $\mu^2$ is the re-scaled invariant mass of the collinear pair, 
\begin{equation}
	\mu^2=\frac{(\kappa_{i_a}+\kappa_{i_b})^2}{\kappa_{ti}^2}\,,\quad \lim_{\mu\to0}(\kappa_{i_a}+\kappa_{i_b})=\kappa_{i}\,.
\end{equation}

The conditions for rIRC safety are the following:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \label{cond:rIRC-a} The limit 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rIRC-a}
\lim_{\bar{v}\to0}\frac{1}{\bar{v}}V(\{\tilde{p}\},\kappa_1(\bar{v}\zeta_1),\dots,\kappa_m(\bar{v}\zeta_m))\,,
\end{equation}
must be finite and non-zero for any choice of non-zero $\zeta_i$. 
The parameter  $\bar{v}$ is introduced to distinguish it from the observable
value $v$ we wish to resum. This requirement ensures that the
 soft-collinear scaling of the observable is independent of the number
 of emissions.
	
\item The following two conditions must hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The addition of a much softer and/or more collinear emission
        must leave the observable unchanged:
\begin{multline}
\label{eq:rIRC-b-i}
\lim_{\zeta_{m+1}\to0}\lim_{\bar{v}\to0}\frac{1}{\bar{v}}
V(\{\tilde{p}\},\kappa_1(\bar{v}\zeta_1),\dots,\kappa_m(\bar{v}\zeta_m),
\kappa_{m+1}(\bar{v}\zeta_{m+1}))\\
=\lim_{\bar{v}\to0}\frac{1}{\bar{v}}V(\{\tilde{p}\},\kappa_1(\bar{v}\zeta_1),\dots,
\kappa_m(\bar{v}\zeta_m))\,.
\end{multline}

\item Analogously, a collinear splitting of an existing emission
        must also leave the observable unchanged:
\begin{multline}
\label{eq:rIRC-b-ii}
\lim_{\mu\to0}\lim_{\bar{v}\to0}\frac{1}{\bar{v}}
V(\{\tilde{p}\},\kappa_1(\bar{v}\zeta_1),\dots,\{\kappa_{i_a},\kappa_{i_b}\}(\bar{v}\zeta_i,\mu),\dots,\kappa_m(\bar{v}\zeta_m))\\
=\lim_{\bar{v}\to0}\frac{1}{\bar{v}}V(\{\tilde{p}\},\kappa_1(\bar{v}\zeta_1),\dots,
\kappa_m(\bar{v}\zeta_m))\,.
\end{multline}
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
The rIRC safety condition is precisely what allows the leading-logarithmic
contributions to exponentiate. Observables that violate rIRC safety do not
exponentiate (cf.\ Eq.~\eqref{eq:resummed-cumulant}), where a well-known example is the \textsc{JADE} 3-jet resolution
parameter in $e^+e^-$ collisions~\cite{Catani:1992tm}. 

\item \textbf{Continuously global}: an observable is said to be
\emph{global} if it is sensitive to emissions in all regions of phase
space, i.e.\ it is non-zero for any additional emission that is not
infinitely soft or collinear. For an observable to be \emph{continuously
global}, its scaling with respect to the transverse momentum of a soft
emission must be uniform across phase space. In terms of the
parametrisation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-sc-V}, this requires the
parameter $a$ to be the same for all legs. This condition ensures 
that non-global logarithms~\cite{Dasgupta:2001sh,Dasgupta:2002dc},
which arise when different regions of phase space exhibit different
transverse-momentum dependences, do not appear. 
Non-global logarithms lie beyond the scope of this thesis.
\end{enumerate}
These properties define the class of observables for which the
\textsc{CAESAR} and \textsc{ARES} resummation formalisms can be applied.
The event-shape observables considered in this thesis satisfy these
requirements. With these properties established, we now turn to the
derivation of the NLL resummation formula.

\section{NLL Resummation}
\label{sec:NLL-resum}

The cumulant for an observable $v$ in a process with hard legs
$\{p\}$ and $n$ real emissions $k_1,\dots,k_n$ is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cumu-gen}
\Sigma\left(\{p\},v\right)=\mathcal{V}(\{p\})\sum_{n=0}^\infty S(n) \int\left(\prod_{i=1}^n\left[dk_i\right]\right)\mathcal{M}^2\left(\{\tilde{p}\},k_1,\dots,k_n\right)\,\Theta\!\left(v-V\left(\{\tilde{p}\},k_1,\dots,k_n\right)\right)\,,
\end{equation}
where: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{V}(\{p\})$ denotes the virtual corrections to the Born process, normalised to the Born matrix element squared,
    \item $S(n)$ is the symmetry factor associated with the $n$-emission configuration,
    \item $[dk]$ denotes the Lorentz-invariant phase space in $d=4-2\varepsilon$ dimensions, 
    \begin{equation}
    [dk] \equiv \frac{d^d k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}} \delta(k^2) \Theta(k^0)\,,
    \end{equation}
    \item $\mathcal{M}^2(\{\tilde{p}\},k_1,\dots,k_n)$ is the squared matrix element for $n$ real emissions off the Born configuration $\{\tilde{p}\}$.
\end{itemize}
We now introduce the approximations that allow Eq.~\eqref{eq:cumu-gen} to be evaluated at NLL
accuracy.

\subsection{Approximations at NLL}
\label{subsec:NLL-approx}

\paragraph{Colour coherence and the physical coupling.}
To simplify the matrix element, we use colour coherence: a soft gluon
emitted at large angles from a set of collinear partons resolves only the
total colour of the collinear ensemble, not the individual colour charges.
This guarantees that emissions widely separated in angle are effectively
independent, so that the squared matrix element for $n$ such emissions
approximately factorises into a product of independent single-emission
contributions.

However, this factorisation is not exact. Correlated gluon clusters, 
in which a primary emission subsequently branches into further gluons, 
give rise to corrections to the single-gluon emission probability. At
NLL, these corrections can be absorbed into a redefinition of the
coupling constant, giving rise to the \emph{physical coupling}
$\alpha_s^{\mathrm{phys}}$, defined as the effective strength of soft
radiation when all subsequent branchings from an initial emission are
included~\cite{Banfi:2018mcq}. This is related to the
$\overline{\mathrm{MS}}$ coupling $\alpha_s$
by\footnote{This scheme is an extension of the CMW scheme of
Ref.~\cite{Catani:1990rr}.}
 \begin{equation} 
 \alpha_s^{\mathrm{phys}} = \alpha_s\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\left(\frac{\alpha_s}{2\pi} \right)^nK^{(n)}\right)\,,
\end{equation} 
where the first two coefficients are 
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:alpha-phys-K}
\begin{align}
    K^{(1)} &= C_A\!\left(\frac{67}{18}-\frac{\pi^2}{6}\right)
               - \frac{5}{9}n_f\,, \\
    K^{(2)} &= C_A^2\!\left(\frac{245}{24} - \frac{67}{9}\zeta_2 
               + \frac{11}{6}\zeta_3 + \frac{11}{5}\zeta_2^2\right)
               + C_F n_f\!\left(-\frac{55}{24} + 2\zeta_3\right)
               \notag \\
             &\quad + C_A n_f\!\left(-\frac{209}{108} 
               + \frac{10}{9}\zeta_2 - \frac{7}{3}\zeta_3\right)
               - \frac{n_f^2}{27}
               + \frac{\pi\beta_0}{2}\!\left(
                 C_A\!\left(\frac{808}{27} - 28\zeta_3\right) 
                 - \frac{224}{54}n_f\right)\,.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Only the first coefficient, $K^{(1)}$, is required at NLL accuracy.

\paragraph{Factorisation of the matrix element.} 
At NLL accuracy, correlated clusters contribute only through the 
coupling redefinition above. The squared matrix element for $n$ widely 
separated soft and collinear emissions factorises into a product of 
$n$ independent single-emission matrix elements in the soft-collinear 
limit, $M_{\mathrm{sc}}^2(k_i)$, as defined in 
Ref.~\cite{Banfi:2014sua}. The observable constraint may likewise be 
taken in the soft-collinear limit,  
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Vsc}
  \frac{\Vsc{k_1,\dots,k_n}}{v}
  \equiv \lim_{v\to 0}\frac{V(\{\tilde{p}\},k_1,\dots,k_n)}{v}\,,
\end{equation}
which is guaranteed to exist for rIRC-safe observables by condition~\ref{cond:rIRC-a}.

At NLL, the virtual corrections exponentiate, 
$\mathcal{V}(\{\tilde{p}\})=e^{-\int[dk]M^2(k)}$, ensuring
that the inclusive sum over all real and virtual contributions equals
unity. Additionally, the $n$ emissions are treated as identical gluons, such that $S(n)=1/n!$, and:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cumu-factor-more}
    \Sigma\left(\{p\},v\right)=e^{-\int[dk]M^2(k)}\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{n!} \int\prod_{i=1}^n\left[dk_i\right]M_{\mathrm{sc}}^2\left(k_i\right)\,\Theta\!\left(v-V_{\mathrm{sc}}\left(\{\tilde{p}\},k_1,\dots,k_n\right)\right)\,.
\end{equation}
To evaluate $\Sigma(\{p\},v)$, we split the exponent in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cumu-factor-more} according to whether the observable value
for a single emission $k$ exceeds $v$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exp-rad}
  \int[dk]\,M^2(k)
  = \underbrace{\int[dk]\,M^2(k)\,\Theta\!\left(V(\{\tilde{p}\},k)-v\right)}_{\displaystyle R(v)}+ \int[dk]\,M_{\mathrm{sc}}^2(k)\,\Theta\!\left(v-V_{\mathrm{sc}}(\{\tilde{p}\},k)\right)\,.
\end{equation}
In the second term, the matrix element and observable are replaced by
their soft-collinear approximations, $M_{\mathrm{sc}}^2$ and
$V_{\mathrm{sc}}$, respectively. At NLL accuracy, only the
soft-collinear approximation is required for this contribution, while
the difference between the full and soft-collinear descriptions gives
subleading corrections that enter at NNLL accuracy.

The first term defines the \emph{radiator} $R(v)$, and the cumulant
factorises as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:cumu-factor-combine}
  \Sigma\left(\{p\},v\right)
  = e^{-R(v)}\,\mathcal{F}(v)\,,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}(v)$ collects the real emissions together with the
virtual corrections from emissions with $V(\{\tilde{p}\},k)\leq v$.
We discuss the radiator $R(v)$ and the multiple-emission function
$\mathcal{F}(v)$ in turn.

The radiator is defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:radiator-NLL}
R(v) \equiv \int[dk]\,M^2(k)\,\Theta\!\left(V(\{\tilde{p}\},k)-v\right)\,,
\end{equation}

$e^{-R(v)}$ is the \emph{Sudakov form factor}, giving the probability that no emission with $V(\{\tilde{p}\},k)>v$ occurs.

At NLL accuracy, the radiator receives contributions from all regions.

\subsubsection{Soft radiator}

\begin{equation}
	R_{\mathrm{s}}=\sum_{\ell}\sum_{\bar{\ell}\neq\ell} C_{\ell\bar{\ell}}\,\mathcal{R}_{\ell\bar\ell}(v)\,,
\end{equation}

C_{\ell\bar{\ell}}=-\mathbf{T}_{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{T}_{\bar\ell}\,.

\begin{equation}

    \sum_{\bar \ell \ne \ell} C_{\ell \bar \ell}= C_\ell\,,

\end{equation}

\subsection{The NLL $\mathcal{F}$-function, $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NLL}}$}
\label{subsec:FNLL}

Having established the radiator, we now turn to the multiple-emission
function $\mathcal{F}(v)$. Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cumu-factor-combine}
and~\eqref{eq:exp-rad}, it is given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Fv-sc}
  \mathcal{F}(v) = e^{-\int[dk]\,M_{\mathrm{sc}}^2(k)\,\Theta(v-V_{\mathrm{sc}}(\{\tilde{p}\},k))}
  \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{1}{n!}
  \int\!\prod_{i=1}^n[dk_i]\,M_{\mathrm{sc}}^2\!\left(k_i\right)
  \,\Theta\!\left(v-V_{\mathrm{sc}}\!\left(\{\tilde{p}\},k_1,\dots,k_n\right)\right)\,.
\end{equation}
To evaluate this expression, it is convenient to express the soft-collinear
matrix element in terms of the radiator derivative. Defining
\begin{equation}
	R'(v)\equiv-v\frac{d}{dv}R(v)\,,
\end{equation}
and introducing, for each emission, the rescaled variable
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zeta-1-emsn}
	\zeta=\frac{V_{\mathrm{sc}}(\{\tilde{p}\},k)}{v}\,,
\end{equation}
we have
\begin{equation}
	\int [dk] M^2(k)=\int_0^\infty \frac{d\zeta}{\zeta} R'(\zeta v)\,,
\end{equation}
and, in particular,
\begin{equation}
\int[dk]\,M^2(k)\,\Theta\!\left(v-V(\{\tilde{p}\},k)\right)=\int_0^1 \frac{d\zeta}{\zeta}\, R'(\zeta v)\,.
\end{equation}
Using the fact that $R'(v)$ decomposes
as a sum over hard legs, $R'(v)\equiv\sum_\ell R'_\ell(v)$,
and changing variables, we obtain 
\begin{equation} 
\label{eq:measure-replace}
[dk]\,M_{\mathrm{sc}}^2(k)=\sum_{\ell}\frac{d\zeta}{\zeta}\frac{d\phi^{(\ell)}}{2\pi}\,R'_{\ell}(\zeta v)\,.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The NLL cumulant}
\label{subsec:NLL-cumulant}

Combining the results of the previous subsections, 
the NLL cumulant takes the form 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:NLL-cumulant}
\Sigma\left(\{p\},v\right)
= e^{-R_{\mathrm{NLL}}(v)}\,
  \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NLL}}\!\left(R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}(v)\right)\,,
\qquad R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}\simeq -v\frac{dR_{\mathrm{NLL}}}{dv}\,,
\end{equation}
where the derivative is evaluated retaining only the terms required for NLL accuracy.

This result applies to any continuously global rIRC-safe observable, and
requires only the parameters $a$, $b_\ell$, $d_\ell$ and $g_\ell(\phi)$ together
with the soft-collinear limit $V_{\mathrm{sc}}$, all of which follow from the
definition of the observable. The existence of this limit is guaranteed by rIRC
safety, and it may be evaluated using suitable ensembles of soft and collinear
emissions.

This procedure is implemented in the \textsc{CAESAR} computer
code~\cite{Banfi:2004yd}, in which emissions are generated by a simplified
parton-shower event generator and passed to a subroutine that evaluates
$V_{\mathrm{sc}}$. The code can also be used to verify the rIRC safety of an
observable directly from its definition in terms of final-state momenta. Its
successor, \textsc{ARES}~\cite{Banfi:2014sua,Banfi:2016zlc,Banfi:2018mcq,Arpino:2019ozn},
extends the framework to NNLL accuracy (cf.\ Ch.~\ref{ch:hhZjet}). The two also
differ in how the multiple-emission function is obtained, with \textsc{CAESAR}
evaluating $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NLL}}$ entirely numerically, whereas
\textsc{ARES} determines $V_{\mathrm{sc}}$ analytically and performs the
remaining integrations by Monte Carlo. Fig.~\ref{fig:FNLL-CAESAR-comp} compares
both against the analytical result for thrust, derived below, and excellent
agreement is observed.

\subsection{Additive observables}
\label{subsec:additive}

A particularly important class of observables are those that are 
\emph{additive}, for which the soft-collinear limit can be
written as a sum of independent contributions from individual
emissions,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:additive-def}
    V_{\mathrm{sc}}(\{\tilde{p}\},k_1,\dots,k_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n V_{\mathrm{sc}}(\{\tilde{p}\},k_i)\,.
\end{equation}
This property allows the multiple-emission function to be evaluated
analytically, where the result is the same for every such observable, with the
observable dependence entering only through the value of $\RpNLL$. Thrust is one
example, as is one-jettiness, the observable studied in Ch.~\ref{ch:hhZjet}.

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:additive-def} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:FNLL-formula}, and
recalling the rescaled variable $\zeta_i$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:zeta-1-emsn}, we
obtain
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:FNLL-add-1}
 \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NLL}}\left(R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}\right)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0} \epsilon^{\RpNLL} \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(\RpNLL)^n}{n!}  \prod_{i=1}^n \left(\int_{\epsilon}^\infty \frac{d\zeta_i}{\zeta_i}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi_i}{2\pi}\right) \Theta\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^n\zeta_i\right)\,,
\end{equation}
where $\RpNLL\equiv \sum_{\ell_i} R'_{\mathrm{NLL},\ell_i}$ since $\phi_i^{(\ell_i)}\to\phi_i$ for
additive observables. \ABquestion{It is true the $\phi$-dependence is the same for each leg?}

The Heaviside function can be expressed using the Mellin transform
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mellin-transform}
    \Theta\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^n\zeta_i\right)=\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}
    \frac{d\nu}{2\pi i\nu} e^\nu\prod_{i=1}^ne^{-\nu\zeta_i}\,, \quad c>0\,,
\end{equation}
so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:FNLL-add-1} becomes,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:FNLL-add-2}
    \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NLL}}\left(R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}\right)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0} \epsilon^{\RpNLL} \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi i \nu}\frac{e^\nu}{\nu}\ \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(\RpNLL)^n}{n!}  \prod_{i=1}^n \left(\int_{\epsilon}^\infty \frac{d\zeta_i}{\zeta_i} \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi_i}{2\pi} e^{-\nu\zeta_i}\right) \,.
\end{equation}
The azimuthal integrals are now trivial, and the sum over $n$ exponentiates,
giving
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:FNLL-add-3}
    \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NLL}}\left(R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}\right)= \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi i \nu}e^\nu \lim_{\epsilon\to 0} \epsilon^{\RpNLL} \exp\left[ \RpNLL \int_{\epsilon}^\infty \frac{d\zeta}{\zeta} e^{-\nu\zeta}\right] \,.
\end{equation}
The limit $\epsilon\to 0$ becomes finite only after combining the prefactor
$\epsilon^{\RpNLL}$ with the exponent. Writing
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon^{\RpNLL}=\exp\left[-\RpNLL\int_{\epsilon}^1\frac{d\zeta}{\zeta}\right]\,,
  \end{equation}
and combining the two exponents leaves the integrand
$e^{-\nu\zeta}-\Theta(1-\zeta)$, which vanishes as $\zeta\to0$. The
limit may now be taken, giving
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:FNLL-add-4}
    \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NLL}}\left(R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}\right)= \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi i \nu}e^\nu \exp\left[ \RpNLL \int_{0}^\infty \frac{d\zeta}{\zeta} \left(e^{-\nu\zeta} - \Theta(1-\zeta)\right)\right] \,.
\end{equation}
The remaining $\zeta$-integral evaluates to
\begin{equation}
	 \int_{0}^\infty \frac{d\zeta}{\zeta} \left(e^{-\nu\zeta} - \Theta(1-\zeta)\right) = -\gamma_E -\ln\nu\,,
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:FNLL-add-5}
    \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NLL}}\left(R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}\right)= e^{-\gamma_E \RpNLL} \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi i\nu}\frac{e^\nu}{\nu^{\RpNLL}}\,.
\end{equation}
Applying the standard inverse Mellin formula
\begin{equation}
    \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi i\,\nu}\,\frac{e^\nu}{\nu^{z}} 
    = \frac{1}{\Gamma(1+z)}\,,
\end{equation}
we arrive at the final result
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:FNLL-add-final}
    \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NLL}}\!\left(R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}\right) 
    = \frac{e^{-\gamma_E R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}}}{\Gamma\!\left(1+R'_{\mathrm{NLL}}\right)}\,,
\end{equation}
in agreement with the numerical results shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:FNLL-CAESAR-comp}.

Having established the NLL resummation framework, we now turn to its
extension to NNLL accuracy provided by the \textsc{ARES} method.
The method was originally developed for $e^+e^-$ annihilation, 
first for jet rates and jet observables in dijet processes~\cite{Banfi:2014sua, Banfi:2016zlc,Banfi:2018mcq},
 and subsequently for event shapes in three-jet production~\cite{Arpino:2019ozn}.
In the following chapter, we extend the \textsc{ARES} method
to hadronic collisions for the first time.
